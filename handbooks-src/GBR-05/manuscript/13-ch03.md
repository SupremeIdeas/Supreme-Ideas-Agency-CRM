## What renting really costs: rent, deposits, fees and bills {.chapter number="3"}

This chapter puts every cost of renting in one place: rent, deposit, fees, bills and the cash you need on day one. By the end you should be able to work out your own move-in budget and spot a payment you should not make.

### What you can lawfully be asked to pay

In England, the Tenant Fees Act 2019 limits what a landlord or agent can charge. Anything that is not on the allowed list is a banned payment. The table shows the allowed payments and their limits.

| **Payment**                                        | **The limit in England**                                              | **Notes**                                                                                                                                        |
|-----------------|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Holding deposit                                    | Up to one week's rent                                                | Reserves the home. Refundable, except where you withdraw, fail the right-to-rent check, give false information or fail to take reasonable steps. |
| Tenancy deposit                                    | Up to five weeks' rent (six weeks if annual rent is £50,000 or more) | Refundable. Must be protected in an approved scheme. The cap covers the whole tenancy, not each joint tenant.                                    |
| Rent in advance                                    | Up to one month's rent, after you sign                               | Nobody may ask for rent before you sign the agreement.                                                                                           |
| Changing the agreement at your request             | Up to £50, or reasonable costs if higher                              | Only if you ask for the change.                                                                                                                  |
| Ending early at your request                       | As agreed in the contract                                             | Check before you ask to leave early.                                                                                                             |
| Utilities, council tax, TV licence, communications | At the real cost                                                      | Only where they are part of the agreement.                                                                                                       |

::: src
Sources: GOV.UK and local council guidance on the Tenant Fees Act 2019; GOV.UK Renters' Rights Act tenant overview, October 2026.
:::

**Banned payments** include administration fees, referencing and credit-check fees, inventory and check-in or check-out fees, and renewal fees [VERIFY: full banned list on GOV.UK]{.v}. A landlord or agent who charges a banned fee can face a penalty of up to £5,000 for a first breach and up to £30,000 or prosecution for further breaches [VERIFY: current penalty levels]{.v}. If you have paid a banned fee, you can ask for it back in writing and report it to your local council's trading standards or private-sector housing team.

**The holding deposit in practice.** Paying a holding deposit takes the home off the market while the landlord checks you. The landlord must give you a decision by an agreed deadline, normally 15 days unless you agree otherwise [VERIFY]{.v}, and must return the holding deposit if you are accepted, normally within seven days, or put it towards your first rent or deposit with your consent [VERIFY]{.v}. Only one holding deposit can be taken for the same home at a time.

### How much cash you need on day one

A deposit of five weeks' rent equals the monthly rent multiplied by 12, divided by 52, then multiplied by 5. Add one month's rent in advance and you have your maximum move-in cash. The table shows the legal maximums for four typical rents. Many landlords ask for less.

| **Monthly rent**             | **Maximum deposit** | **One month's rent** | **Move-in cash (approx. US\$)** |
|--------------------------|---------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Room in a shared house, £650 | £750                | £650                  | £1,400 (US\$1,848)              |
| Flat at £1,000               | £1,154              | £1,000                | £2,154 (US\$2,843)              |
| England average, £1,459      | £1,683              | £1,459                | £3,142 (US\$4,147)              |
| London average, £2,332       | £2,691              | £2,332                | £5,023 (US\$6,630)              |

*Maximums under the English rules at £1 = US\$1.32. Excludes council tax, bills, any guarantor fee, furniture and travel. A holding deposit is not an extra cost; it is taken off the deposit or first rent.*

At planning rates of £1 = ₦1,790, the £2,154 move-in cash for a £1,000 flat is about ₦3.86 million. Plan to have more than the bare maximum, because your first weeks also include food, transport, a phone and household basics (see Land and Live in the UK, GBR-01).

### Your deposit: where it goes and how to get it back

In England and Wales, a landlord or agent who takes a deposit must place it in a government-approved scheme within 30 days and give you the scheme's details in writing. The approved schemes are the Deposit Protection Service (DPS), mydeposits and the Tenancy Deposit Scheme [VERIFY: current approved schemes]{.v}. The scheme holds the money, or insures it, and runs a free dispute service if you and the landlord disagree about deductions at the end. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own schemes and deadlines (Chapter 1).

A landlord may deduct for damage beyond **fair wear and tear**, unpaid rent or unpaid bills, but not for the ordinary ageing of a home. Protect yourself on day one: photograph and date every room, the meter readings, and any damage, then send the photos to the landlord by email the same day. Chapter 14 covers the inventory in detail, and Chapter 18 covers getting your deposit back.

::: {.plain title="Fair wear and tear"}
Fair wear and tear is the normal ageing of a home that comes from people living in it, such as lightly faded paint or a carpet that has flattened in a busy corridor. It is different from damage, such as a burn mark or a broken door. Landlords cannot take money from your deposit for fair wear and tear.
:::

### No UK credit history? Guarantors and the end of the six-month trick

Newcomers often have good jobs and no UK credit history, which makes landlords nervous. For years the common workaround was to pay six or even twelve months of rent in advance. From 1 May 2026, in most private lets in England, landlords and agents cannot ask for more than one month's rent in advance, and cannot take rent before the agreement is signed. Larger advance payments can still be accepted in some purpose-built student accommodation [VERIFY: which student and other lets are exempt]{.v}.

That leaves four practical routes for a newcomer.

1.  **A UK-based personal guarantor.** Usually someone who lives in the UK, earns enough and passes a credit check, which rules out many relatives and friends back home.

2.  **A professional guarantor service.** A company that agrees to guarantee your rent for a fee. Accepted by many landlords and agents, but not all [VERIFY: fees and acceptance]{.v}.

3.  **Strong evidence of income and savings.** A signed job offer or contract, recent payslips, bank statements and proof of savings can persuade a landlord, even without a UK credit record.

4.  **A landlord or agent who works with international tenants.** Some specialise in new arrivals and have their own checks.

If anyone outside student housing still demands several months of rent up front, treat it as a red flag. It may be unlawful, or it may be a scam. Ask for the demand in writing, and if you are in doubt, contact Citizens Advice or your local council before you pay.

### The monthly bills that sit on top of rent

Check every home's bills before you commit. Some rents include bills, many do not, and council tax can be the biggest surprise. The ranges below are editorial planning estimates for a one-person household, not quotes.

|------------------|-------------|--------------|---------------------------|
| Energy (gas and electricity) | £100 to £160      | US\$132 to US\$211 | Ofgem's cap is £1,723 a year for a typical household from 1 October to 31 December 2026, about £144 a month.                                                                              |
| Water and sewerage           | £30 to £60        | US\$40 to US\$79   | Varies by area and meter [\[VERIFY\]]{.v}.                                                                                                                                                 |
| Council tax                  | £0 to £200        | US\$0 to US\$264   | Depends on band and area; average Band D in England in 2026/27 is about £2,392 a year. Full-time student households can be exempt, and a single adult can get a discount [\[VERIFY\]]{.v}. |
| Broadband                    | £25 to £40        | US\$33 to US\$53   | Often a 12 to 24-month contract.                                                                                                                                                           |
| Mobile plan                  | £10 to £25        | US\$13 to US\$33   | Compare SIM-only plans.                                                                                                                                                                    |
| Contents insurance           | £5 to £15         | US\$7 to US\$20    | Covers your belongings, not the landlord's building.                                                                                                                                      |

### Step by step: build your move-in budget

1.  **Fix the rent.** Choose a ceiling from Chapter 1 and confirm it passes the income test or that you have a guarantor.

2.  **Calculate the deposit** as rent × 12 ÷ 52 × 5, and add one month's rent.

3.  **List the monthly bills** from the table and ask each landlord which are included.

4.  **Add one-off costs:** a guarantor fee if you use one, transport to the home, basic household items and bedding.

5.  **Keep a cushion** equal to at least one extra month of rent, so a delay does not become an emergency.

::: {.example title="The real monthly cost"}
A nurse is moving from Nairobi to a city in the English Midlands and finds a one-bedroom flat at £1,000 a month. The move-in cash is £2,154: a deposit of £1,154 plus the first month's rent. Monthly costs are rent £1,000, energy £120, water £40, council tax £140, broadband £30, mobile £15 and contents insurance £8, a total of £1,353 (US\$1,786). The real cost of the home is about 35% above the advertised rent. The nurse also budgets a guarantor service fee [VERIFY: fee]{.v} and a month's cushion, so the cash needed before the first pay is higher than the day-one maximum.
:::

### Paying safely

Pay deposits and rent only by bank transfer to an account in the name of the landlord or the agent company, and only after you have verified who they are. Match the account name to the name on the agreement. Be wary of requests to pay in cash, by gift card, by cryptocurrency, by money-transfer app to a person, or to an account in someone else's name. Get a written receipt every time, and keep a record of the date, amount and reference. Chapter 9 explains the scams that depend on you paying quickly.

::: {.checklist title="Payment check before i transfer any money"}
-   The payment type is on the allowed list (holding deposit, deposit, rent in advance).
-   The amount is within the legal limit for the rent.
-   I have seen the home or had it verified, and I know who the landlord is.
-   The account name matches the landlord or the agent company.
-   I will receive a written receipt, and the deposit scheme details within 30 days.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

-   Paying a holding deposit before checking that you meet the guarantor and right-to-rent requirements, and then losing it if you fail those checks.

-   Budgeting for rent and deposit but forgetting council tax, which can be one of the largest monthly costs.

-   Paying a "reservation fee" or "admin fee" that is not on the allowed list.

-   Paying a deposit to a person rather than to the agent company or the landlord named in the agreement.

-   Not photographing the home on day one, then losing money from your deposit for damage that was already there.
