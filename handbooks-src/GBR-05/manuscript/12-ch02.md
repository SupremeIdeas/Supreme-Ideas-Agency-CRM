## The law on your side: tenancies and the Renters' Rights Act {.chapter number="2"}

This chapter explains what changed in English private renting on 1 May 2026 and what the law now says you can and cannot be asked to do. By the end you should be able to tell a lawful request from an unlawful one before you sign anything.

### What you need to know

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 is the biggest change to private renting in England in decades. It applies to England only. It is being introduced in phases, and the first and largest phase took effect on 1 May 2026. The table gives the dates that matter.

| **Date**        | **What happened or happens**                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
|--------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| 27 October 2025 | The Renters' Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent.                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| 1 May 2026      | Phase 1 in force in England: Section 21 "no-fault" evictions abolished, tenancies become periodic, new rent rules, ban on bidding, rent-in-advance limit, ban on discrimination against children and benefits, right to request a pet. |
| 31 May 2026     | Deadline for landlords to give existing tenants the government's information sheet.                                                                                                                                                     |
| 31 July 2026    | Last date to start court action on an old Section 21 notice served before 1 May 2026.                                                                                                                                                    |
| From late 2026  | Phase 2: a national landlord database starts rolling out, with a landlord ombudsman to follow. Exact dates still to be confirmed.                                                                                                        |
| Later           | Phase 3: further standards, including the Decent Homes Standard and Awaab's Law for private rented homes. Dates to be set after consultation.                                                                                           |

### What the Act does for you as a tenant

-   **Your tenancy has no end date.** Private tenancies in England are now periodic: they roll on month to month (or week to week, depending on how rent is paid) until you leave or a landlord ends the tenancy with a legal reason. Your tenancy will not run out on a fixed date.

-   **You can leave by giving two months' notice.** You are not locked into a twelve-month term. Give the notice in writing, and time it so that it ends on a rent-due date (the official guidance says to give it on the rent due date or the day before). You must keep paying rent during your notice period, and you can agree a shorter notice with your landlord in writing.

-   **No more no-fault eviction.** Landlords can no longer use a Section 21 notice. To regain a home they must use a Section 8 notice and rely on a specific legal ground, such as unpaid rent, serious anti-social behaviour, or the landlord selling or moving in. If you do not leave, the landlord must go to court [VERIFY: current grounds and notice periods]{.v}.

-   **Rent can rise once a year, with notice.** Not in the first year of a new tenancy, and only with at least two months' written notice on the correct form (a Section 13 notice). If you think the new rent is above the market rate, you can challenge it at the First-tier Tribunal.

-   **Rent in advance is capped at one month.** Nobody may ask for, encourage or accept rent before you have signed the tenancy agreement, and after you sign you can be asked for a maximum of one month's rent in advance.

-   **No bidding wars.** Landlords and agents cannot accept or encourage offers above the advertised rent. The advertised price is the price.

-   **No blanket refusals for children or benefits.** A landlord or agent cannot do anything that makes it less likely you will get a home because you have children or receive benefits. Discrimination because of a protected characteristic under the Equality Act is also unlawful.

-   **You can ask to keep a pet.** A landlord must consider your request and cannot refuse unreasonably.

-   **You should get the terms in writing.** New tenancies should come with a written agreement setting out key terms and your rights. Landlords had to give existing tenants an official information sheet by 31 May 2026.

### What did not change

-   **Right to rent checks continue.** In England, landlords must still check that every adult tenant has the legal right to rent (Chapter 7). The Act did not abolish these checks.

-   **The Tenant Fees Act still applies.** Most letting fees remain banned, and deposits remain capped (Chapter 3).

-   **Deposits must still be protected** in an approved scheme within 30 days (Chapter 3).

-   **Landlords can still end a tenancy** for proper reasons, including serious rent arrears. If you stop paying rent, you can still be evicted.

-   **Some accommodation is outside the new rules or follows different ones**, for example certain licences and some student accommodation [VERIFY: GOV.UK list of accommodation types covered by the Act]{.v}. A student can also be asked to leave an HMO at the end of the academic year under a specific ground for student houses.

::: {.plain title="Three terms you will meet"}
An **assured periodic tenancy** is the standard private tenancy in England: it rolls on until it is ended lawfully. A **Section 8 notice** is how a landlord asks a tenant to leave, based on a stated legal ground. A **Section 13 notice** (Form 4A) is the official form a landlord must use to propose a rent increase.
:::

### When a landlord does not follow the rules: who enforces the law

The Act gives councils and tribunals real powers, so you are not left to argue alone. In England, local councils can investigate landlords and agents, issue penalty notices and prosecute serious breaches. A council housing or "private sector housing" team is usually the right first call for an unsafe home, an unlicensed house in multiple occupation or a banned fee. For some offences, such as renting out a property that should be licensed, you can apply to the First-tier Tribunal for a **Rent Repayment Order**, which can order a landlord to repay up to two years of rent [VERIFY: which offences qualify and the current maximum]{.v}.

| Problem | First contact | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Landlord or agent charges a banned fee | Write to them asking for it back | Local council trading standards or housing team |
| Unsafe or damp home | Landlord, in writing | Council environmental health or private housing team |
| Landlord tries to evict without a court order | Council housing team and Shelter's helpline | Police, if you are locked out; a solicitor |
| Rent increase that looks too high | Landlord, in writing | First-tier Tribunal (property chamber) before the increase takes effect |
| You are unsure whether a payment is lawful | Citizens Advice or Shelter | Your local council |

### Step by step: check your rights before you sign

1.  **Confirm the home is in England.** If it is in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland, the rules in Chapter 1's table apply instead.

2.  **Ask for the written agreement before you pay a deposit,** and read it. Check the rent, the rent period, who pays which bills, the deposit amount and the notice you must give.

3.  **Compare every payment with the allowed list** in Chapter 3. If a payment is not on the list, ask the landlord or agent to explain in writing.

4.  **Check who you are dealing with.** Get the landlord's name and address, and the agent's company name and redress scheme (Chapter 7).

5.  **Ask which deposit scheme will hold your deposit,** and ask for the scheme's details within 30 days of paying.

6.  **Keep copies.** Photograph every page you sign, save emails and keep receipts in one folder.

1\. Is the home in England? If no, use the rules for Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland (Chapter 1).

3\. Is it a university hall or purpose-built student accommodation? If yes, check your licence or contract, and Chapter 12 [VERIFY]{.v}.

4\. Is it a normal private let from a landlord or agent? If yes, the Renters' Rights Act rules in this chapter apply. Check the allowed payments in Chapter 3.

The written reply did two things: it showed the tenant knew the rules, and it created a record.

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

-   Paying months of rent up front because "everyone does". Outside student accommodation and a few other cases, that is no longer lawful in England [VERIFY: exceptions]{.v}.

-   Assuming a twelve-month agreement locks you in. You can leave on two months' notice, but you still owe rent until the notice ends.

-   Ignoring a rent-increase letter. If the notice is wrong or the rent is above market, you can challenge it, but there are time limits [VERIFY: tribunal time limits]{.v}.

-   Believing a landlord who says you have "no rights" because you are new to the UK. Your tenancy rights do not depend on how long you have lived here.

-   Stopping rent because of a dispute. Withholding rent can give the landlord a legal ground to evict you. Get advice first.
