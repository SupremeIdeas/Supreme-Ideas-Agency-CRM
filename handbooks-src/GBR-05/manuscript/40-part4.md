# Where and what to rent {.part number="IV" sub="How to choose a region and a neighbourhood, which type of home suits you, and how the rules change in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland."}

## Choosing a region and a neighbourhood {.chapter number="11"}

This chapter helps you choose where to live. It compares the main parts of the UK, shows how to weigh rent against travel, and gives you a checklist for judging a street before you sign. By the end you should be able to rank three areas for your own life, not just for the price.

### What you need to know

The cheapest rent is rarely the cheapest home. A room 90 minutes from work costs you time, fares and energy every day. A slightly dearer home near a station can save you hours and money. Choose the area first, and the specific home second.

The latest official figures show how much the average rent differs between regions. London averages about £2,332 a month, the North East about £788, Wales about £846 and Scotland about £1,013 (ONS, August 2026, Chapter 1). The other regions of England sit between these, and a city centre costs more than the suburbs around it [VERIFY: the latest ONS regional table]{.v}.

### The five tests for a neighbourhood

1. **The commute test.** Do the journey at the time you will make it, on a weekday, and add the cost of a season ticket. Look at the last train or bus home.
2. **The cost test.** Add rent, council tax and travel for each area. A cheaper room that adds £120 a month in fares may not be cheaper.
3. **The safety test.** Visit in daylight and at night. Check the local police crime map on police.uk and ask neighbours and shopkeepers [VERIFY: the police.uk crime map]{.v}.
4. **The life test.** Is there food from home, a place of worship, a health centre, a park and a community that suits you?
5. **The services test.** Check the nearest GP surgery, school catchment if you have children and broadband speeds.

### Council tax, transport and hidden differences

Council tax varies widely between councils for the same band. A home in one borough can cost far more per year than the same band in the next. Transport zones, parking permits and local congestion charges can also change the real cost. Compare the **total monthly cost** of each area, not just the rent (Chapter 3).

| Cost | How it varies |
|---|---|
| Council tax | By council and band; full-time students are exempt, and a single adult can get a discount. |
| Transport | By city. London has zones and caps, other cities have bus and tram passes, and rail season tickets vary. |
| Energy | By region and by the home's EPC rating. |
| Parking | Resident permits and controlled zones can be expensive. |
| Broadband | By provider coverage; check before you sign. |

### Choosing with family

If you are moving with a partner or children, add three more checks. Look at the schools' Ofsted or equivalent reports, and the admissions rules for school places, which can depend on your address and timing [VERIFY: school admissions timetable]{.v}. Check whether the home allows children and what the landlord says about noise. Ask whether nurseries and childcare have places and cost what you expect.

::: {.example title="Comparing three areas"}
A nurse with a job at a hospital in Leeds compares three options. A room in a house share in the city centre costs £620 and is a 15-minute walk from work. A one-bedroom flat in a suburb costs £820, with a 25-minute bus ride that costs £60 a month. A studio further out costs £680 but needs a train and bus, with £130 a month in fares and a 70-minute journey. She chooses the house share for the first six months because it has the lowest total cost and time, then plans to move to the suburban flat when she has made friends and found her feet.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Neighbourhood checklist"}
- I have done the commute at the real time and priced it.
- I know the council tax band and the total monthly cost.
- I have visited in the day and at night.
- I have found food, worship, health and community near the home.
- I know the broadband options and the EPC rating.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Choosing an area from a photo and a price.
- Ignoring the cost and time of the commute.
- Forgetting council tax differences between neighbouring areas.
- Visiting only in the daytime.
- Choosing a home far from your community and support.

## Types of home: rooms, flats, shared houses and student places {.chapter number="12"}

This chapter explains the main types of home in the UK, what each costs and the rules that apply, including houses in multiple occupation, lodgers and student accommodation. By the end you should know which type suits your budget and your situation.

### What you need to know

The type of home changes your rights, your costs and your daily life. A room in a shared house is cheaper and quicker to get, but you may share a kitchen with strangers. A one-bedroom flat gives privacy, but it costs more and needs stronger references. A lodger arrangement is fast and flexible, but it comes with fewer legal protections.

| Type | Typical features | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Room in a shared house or flat | A private bedroom with a shared kitchen and bathroom; bills often split | Newcomers, students, single workers | You may be one of several tenants on one agreement (joint liability); check licensing. |
| Studio | One room with a kitchenette and bathroom | Single people who want privacy | Higher cost per square metre and often a stricter income test. |
| One- or two-bedroom flat | Self-contained; furnished or unfurnished | Couples, small families, professionals | The council tax, bills and deposit are all yours. |
| House | Often for families or groups of friends | Families and groups | Large deposit and maintenance responsibilities. |
| Purpose-built student accommodation (PBSA) and university halls | Rooms in blocks with shared or private facilities, often bills included | Students | Different rules and contracts; see below. |
| Lodging (a room in the landlord's own home) | You share space with the landlord | Newcomers who need a quick start | Fewer legal protections and house rules. |
| Build-to-rent apartments | Large, professionally managed blocks with services | Professionals who want amenities | Higher rents and service charges. |
| Property guardianship | A cheap licence to live in an empty building | Short-term needs | Very limited rights; can end on short notice. |
| Serviced apartments and short lets | A furnished apartment with hotel-style services | The first weeks while you search | Expensive per night; not for the long term. |

### Shared houses and HMOs

A house in multiple occupation (HMO) is a property let to three or more people who are not from one household and who share facilities. Larger HMOs must be licensed by the council, and licensed homes must meet higher standards for safety and space. In England, a property occupied by five or more people from two or more households needs a mandatory licence, and many councils also require licences for smaller shared houses [VERIFY: your council's licensing scheme]{.v}.

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Licence | Ask for the licence number and check the council's public register. |
| Room size | A licensed bedroom for one adult must be at least 6.51 square metres in England [VERIFY: current minimum sizes]{.v}. |
| Fire safety | Smoke alarms, fire doors, escape routes and a fire blanket in the kitchen. |
| Number of people | The licence states the maximum number of occupants. |
| Facilities | Enough bathrooms and a safe kitchen for the number of tenants. |
| Agreement | One joint agreement (joint and several liability) or individual agreements? |
| Bills | Included or split? Who is the account holder? |

::: {.warn}
An unlicensed HMO that should be licensed is a sign of a landlord who may cut corners. In England, you may be able to apply for a Rent Repayment Order to recover up to two years of rent if a landlord has let an unlicensed property that should have been licensed (Chapter 2).
:::

### Renting as a lodger

A lodger rents a room in the landlord's own home. In England, lodgers who share living space with their landlord have fewer rights than other tenants. The landlord must usually give "reasonable notice" to end the arrangement, and you cannot normally be evicted without notice, but you do not have the full protection of an assured tenancy [VERIFY: your rights as a lodger]{.v}. Landlords can earn up to £7,500 a year tax-free under the Rent a Room scheme [VERIFY: current limit]{.v}.

- **Good points:** Quick, flexible, often bills included, helpful for the first weeks in a new country.
- **Cautions:** Agree house rules, guests, privacy and notice in writing. Check that the landlord is allowed to take a lodger (their mortgage lender and insurer may need to agree). Do the right-to-rent check.

### Student accommodation

Students have special options, including university halls and PBSA. Halls are managed by the university, and PBSA by private companies. They often include bills and internet, and international students can book from abroad. The right-to-rent check may not apply to some student accommodation, but you must still check the contract [VERIFY: current exemptions]{.v}. The companion book *Study in the UK* (GBR-04) covers halls, private blocks and shared houses for students in detail, and an English student HMO can be ended by the landlord at the end of the academic year, under a specific ground.

### Furnished or unfurnished, and what to ask about

- **Furnished:** beds, wardrobe, sofa, table, and often kitchen appliances. Easier on arrival, but check the inventory and who owns the items.
- **Unfurnished:** you provide everything. Cheaper in rent but costly to set up.
- **Part-furnished:** typically includes white goods.

Ask the landlord what is included in writing, and who maintains each item.

### A quick decision guide

1. **Do you want to move in this week?** A room in a shared house or a short stay is usually fastest.
2. **Do you have a stable income and a guarantor?** You can consider a flat.
3. **Do you value privacy above price?** A studio or a one-bedroom flat.
4. **Are you a student?** Start with halls or a PBSA, then a shared house in year two.
5. **Are you moving with family?** A flat or house, with schools and space in mind.

::: {.example title="Choosing a first home"}
A software tester from Lagos arrives with a three-month contract and no UK guarantor. She chooses a licensed room in a house share for £640 a month with bills included. The agent asks for a holding deposit, her passport and share code, and a reference from her employer. She is on a joint agreement with three housemates, so she asks about the others and reads the clause about shared liability. After three months she moves to a studio on her own, with a professional guarantor and payslips.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Signing for a shared house without checking the licence.
- Assuming the deposit is yours alone in a joint tenancy.
- Choosing a lodger arrangement without agreeing notice.
- Accepting a property-guardian licence without understanding the limits.
- Not asking what is included in a furnished property.

## Renting in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland {.chapter number="13"}

This chapter explains how the rules differ outside England. By the end you should know the main differences in tenancy type, deposits, notice and landlord registration, and where to find the official guidance for your nation.

### What you need to know

Housing law is devolved, so Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules. In many ways, they are more protective of tenants than England was before 2026, and in some ways they differ in technical details. The key lesson is this: **look up the rules for the nation where the home is**, and do not rely on an English guide.

| Topic | Wales | Scotland | Northern Ireland |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tenancy type | Occupation contract (standard contract) | Private Residential Tenancy (open-ended) | Private tenancy |
| What you are called | Contract-holder | Tenant | Tenant |
| Deposit cap | No fixed legal cap [VERIFY]{.v} | Two months' rent | One month's rent |
| Deposit protection | Approved scheme within 30 days [VERIFY]{.v} | Approved scheme within 30 working days | Approved scheme within 28 days |
| Landlord registration | Landlords must register and be licensed through Rent Smart Wales [VERIFY]{.v} | Landlords must be on the Scottish landlord register | Landlords must register with the Landlord Registration Scheme |
| Rent increases | Once a year, with notice [VERIFY]{.v} | Once every 12 months with notice; rent adjudication [VERIFY]{.v} | Once every 12 months, with notice [VERIFY]{.v} |
| Ending a tenancy | Landlord notice and grounds; tenant gives one month's notice [VERIFY]{.v} | Landlord needs one of the legal grounds; tenant gives 28 days' notice [VERIFY]{.v} | Notice to quit periods depend on the length of the tenancy [VERIFY]{.v} |
| Letting fees | Banned in most cases | Banned | Banned in most cases |

::: {.src}
Compiled from the Welsh Government, Scottish Government, NI Direct and Housing Rights guidance, October 2026. Details change, and each row needs checking against the official source for your nation.
:::

### Wales

The Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016 replaced tenancies with occupation contracts. You get a written statement of the contract, and the landlord must provide it within 14 days. The home must be "fit for human habitation", and landlords and agents must be registered and licensed. Wales has its own rules on notices, so check the Welsh Government's guidance and Shelter Cymru.

### Scotland

Scotland's Private Residential Tenancy is open-ended and cannot be ended without one of a list of legal grounds. Letting agents cannot charge fees to tenants, and deposits are protected in one of three approved schemes. Landlords must be on the landlord register, and homes must meet the Repairing Standard. Scotland has been developing rent-control powers for some areas, so check the Scottish Government's pages for the latest [VERIFY: rent-control areas and rules]{.v}. Shelter Scotland and Citizens Advice Scotland are the key advice services.

### Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland's Private Tenancies Act 2022 introduced a cap of one month's rent on deposits, limited rent increases and set notice-to-quit rules based on the length of the tenancy. Landlords must register, and deposits must be protected in an approved scheme within 28 days [VERIFY: current rules and the status of longer notice periods]{.v}. Housing Rights is the main advice service.

### If you move between nations

If you rent in one nation and move to another, do not assume that anything carries over. Check:

1. **The tenancy type and your notice rights** in the new nation.
2. **The deposit cap, the scheme and the time limits.**
3. **The landlord and agent registration,** and look them up on the national registers.
4. **The repairs standard** and who to contact for enforcement.
5. **The local council's housing team** and the national advice charity.

::: {.example title="Checking the right nation"}
A teacher accepts a job in Cardiff after living in Manchester. In Manchester her landlord took a deposit of five weeks' rent. In Cardiff, the agent asks for a deposit of two months' rent. She checks the Welsh Government's guidance, sees that there is no fixed cap in Wales, and asks the agent to justify the amount. The agent explains that the property allows pets, and she agrees to a deposit of six weeks' rent in exchange for a written pet clause. She also checks that the landlord is on Rent Smart Wales.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Using English rules in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland.
- Not checking landlord or agent registration.
- Missing a deposit scheme deadline because you assumed it matched England.
- Ignoring a nation's own advice charity.
