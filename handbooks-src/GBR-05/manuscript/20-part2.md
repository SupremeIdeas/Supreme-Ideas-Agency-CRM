# Finding and securing a home {.part number="II" sub="Where to search, how to view, what landlords check, the papers you need and what to read before you sign."}

## Searching: where to look and how to move fast {.chapter number="4"}

This chapter explains where UK homes are advertised, how each source differs in price and risk, and how to search quickly and safely. By the end you should have a short list of trusted sources, a set of saved searches and a routine that gets you in front of good homes first.

### What you need to know

Most UK rentals are advertised online. Good homes in popular areas can disappear within hours, so the person who replies first with complete information often wins. Speed matters, and so does safety. The most expensive mistakes in renting begin with a listing that looked perfect.

| Source | Best for | Typical risk | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large property portals (Rightmove, Zoopla, OnTheMarket) | Flats and houses from agents and landlords | Low to medium | Most listings come from regulated agents. Scam listings exist, so verify. |
| Direct-landlord platforms (OpenRent and similar) | Cheaper rents, direct contact with landlords | Medium | You deal with one person. Check identity and ownership. |
| Room-share platforms (SpareRoom and similar) | Rooms in shared houses and flats | Medium to high | Many scams use this market. Never pay before viewing. |
| University accommodation sites and approved-landlord lists | Students | Low | Landlords are vetted; see Chapter 12. |
| Local letting agents' own sites and shop windows | Local homes before they reach portals | Low | Visit in person, ask to join the mailing list. |
| Social media groups and classifieds | Quick room-shares and lodgers | High | The highest scam rate. Treat as a last resort, and see Chapter 9. |
| Employer relocation services and community networks | Newcomers with a job offer | Low to medium | Ask your employer's HR team, your community or faith group, and alumni networks. |

::: {.src}
Editorial comparison, October 2026. Risk reflects how often scams appear, not the honesty of any one advertiser.
:::


### Build your search in five steps

1. **Define the search:** area, maximum rent, minimum bedrooms and the date you need to move in. Chapter 11 helps you choose areas.
2. **Save the search and turn on instant alerts** on two portals, so you hear about a new listing within minutes.
3. **Prepare a standard message** (see below) so you can reply in 60 seconds with your name, move-in date, work or study and when you can view.
4. **Keep a spreadsheet** of every home: address, rent, agent, date contacted, answers and a verdict.
5. **Compare against the market** by pricing each home against two or three similar ones nearby. A rent far below the market is a warning sign, not a bargain.

### A message that gets replies

Agents get dozens of messages for each good home. A clear, short message stands out.

> Hello, I am interested in (address). I am (a nurse / engineer / student) starting at (employer or university) on (date), and I can move in from (date). I can view on (days and times), in person or by video. My monthly income is (amount) and I have (a guarantor / savings / a job contract). Please tell me the rent, the deposit, the tenancy start date and what is included. Thank you, (name, phone number).

### Reading a listing like a professional

Every UK advert carries clues. Read these parts first.

| Look for | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Rent and rent period | Per calendar month (pcm) or per week (pw). Multiply a weekly rent by 52 and divide by 12 to get the monthly figure. |
| Deposit | In England it must not exceed five weeks' rent for most homes. |
| Available from | The date you can move in. A very early or "immediate" date may mean a rush. |
| EPC rating | The Energy Performance Certificate rating from A to G. A poor rating means higher bills. |
| Council tax band | A to H in England and Scotland, and the band decides the bill. |
| Furnished or unfurnished | Affects your move-in costs. |
| Floor plan and photos | A missing floor plan or stock photos may mean a weak listing or a scam. |
| Agent name and branch | You should be able to find the agent's website, address and phone number. |
| Pets, sharers and DSS wording | Banned blanket refusals are unlawful in England (Chapter 2). |

::: {.insight}
When you are still abroad, you may miss calls from UK numbers. Use a data eSIM and a virtual UK number, such as those offered by Naara, so that agents can reach you and you can reply to viewing messages on the day. Test it before you start searching. Keep your home number for banking codes.
:::

### Searching from abroad

You can search from abroad, but you should not sign for a home you have not seen unless someone you trust has viewed it, or you are renting through a regulated agent that offers a live video tour and a verified contract. For the first week, book a short stay and finish the search in person. Chapter 9 explains why.

::: {.example title="A fast, safe search"}
A software engineer lands in Leeds on Friday, with a hotel booked for ten nights. By Sunday she has saved searches on two portals, viewed five flats in person on Saturday and Monday and asked each agent the same questions. She chooses a flat at £950 a month on Monday, completes the application on Tuesday, pays the holding deposit by bank transfer to the agent's company account and signs on the following Monday. She never paid anything before she saw the home.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Searching only on social media.
- Replying to a listing with a rent far below the market, and paying "to hold" it.
- Not saving searches and alerts, so you are always third in line.
- Ignoring the council tax band and the EPC rating.
- Waiting until you land to start searching.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: the agent's registration with a redress scheme and client money protection, the landlord's name against the Land Registry or the local council's records where possible, and the current rules on advertising rent in England. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

## Viewings: what to check and what to ask {.chapter number="5"}

This chapter explains how to arrange and use a viewing, what to inspect, the questions to ask and the red flags to leave on. By the end you should be able to judge a home in 20 minutes and avoid a costly mistake.

### What you need to know

A viewing is your one chance to see the real home before you commit. Treat it like an inspection, not a visit. Go prepared, go in person if you can and ask every question on your list.

::: {.plain title="Never pay before you see it"}
Do not pay a deposit, holding fee or any money to see a home or to "secure" it before you have viewed it or had it checked by someone you trust. Real agents let you view first, and they take a holding deposit only after you have applied (Chapter 6).
:::

### Before the viewing

1. **Confirm the viewing in writing,** with the address, the time, the name of the person who will meet you and their phone number.
2. **Check the person and the company.** For an agent, find their website and registration. For a private landlord, ask for their name and check it against the listing and the Land Registry where possible [VERIFY: how to check ownership on the Land Registry]{.v}.
3. **Tell a friend where you are going,** and share your location.
4. **Take your phone, a torch app, a tape measure and your checklist.** Keep your documents in your bag, not in the property.
5. **Plan the journey,** and do it at the time you will commute, to test it.

### What to inspect

| Area | What to check |
|---|---|
| Entrance and security | Door locks, entry system, lighting, signs of break-ins, communal areas. |
| Damp and mould | Black or green patches on walls and ceilings, musty smell, peeling paint, condensation on windows. |
| Windows and doors | Do they open and close, lock and seal? Draughts? |
| Heating and hot water | Boiler age and type, radiators, thermostat, hot water on demand. |
| Electrics | Number of sockets, loose plates, visible scorch marks, the consumer unit. |
| Kitchen and bathroom | Leaks, extraction, working appliances, water pressure. |
| Safety | Smoke alarms on each floor and carbon monoxide alarms near fuel-burning appliances. |
| Space and storage | Room size, wardrobes, bike storage, bins. |
| Noise and light | Visit at different times, listen at the windows, check what the neighbours are like. |
| Signal and broadband | Check your phone signal and ask which broadband providers serve the address. |
| The street | Parking, transport, shops, lighting at night. |


### Questions to ask the agent or landlord

- What is the rent, the deposit and the holding deposit, and what is the move-in date?
- Which bills are included, and what are the typical costs (energy, water, council tax band, broadband)?
- Which deposit scheme will protect my deposit?
- Is there a current gas safety certificate and an electrical safety report (EICR)? What is the EPC rating?
- Who is the landlord, and who manages the property? Where do I report repairs?
- How long has the current tenant lived here, and why are they leaving?
- What are the rules on guests, pets and decorating?
- What will you check about me (income, references, guarantor, right to rent)?
- When will I get a decision, and what will happen to my holding deposit?

::: {.warn}
Leave if you are asked to pay in cash on the spot, if the person cannot show who they are, if the home looks different from the photos, if you are pressured to sign today, or if you are told the "keys are with someone else" and you must pay first. Real viewings do not need these.
:::

### Virtual viewings and viewing from abroad

A live video tour with an agent can be a good first filter, but it cannot show smell, noise, damp, neighbours or what is outside the camera. Ask the agent to walk to the street, show the front door, the entry system, the bathroom and any damp. Record the call if the agent agrees, and ask for a floor plan and the safety certificates in writing. If you cannot view in person, ask someone you trust, such as a relative, a colleague or a community contact, to visit.

### After the viewing

1. **Write notes straight away,** while you remember.
2. **Compare homes in your spreadsheet.**
3. **Ask the questions you forgot** by email, so that the answers are in writing.
4. **Decide quickly but not blindly.** If you want it, apply the same day (Chapter 6).

::: {.example title="A viewing that saved a deposit"}
A teacher views a flat with a low rent. The photos show a bright living room, but in person she smells damp, sees a black patch behind a wardrobe and notices that the smoke alarm has no battery. The agent says it is "just condensation". She asks for the EICR and gas safety certificate, and the agent cannot produce them. She leaves, applies for another flat the same day, and avoids a winter of cold and mould.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Viewing checklist"}
- I confirmed the address, time and person in writing.
- I checked the agent or landlord's identity.
- I told a friend and shared my location.
- I checked damp, heating, safety, windows, kitchen and bathroom.
- I asked about bills, deposit scheme, certificates and the decision date.
- I took photos and notes.
- I did not pay anything at the viewing.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Paying to "hold" a home you have not seen.
- Viewing only in daylight and missing noise or safety issues.
- Taking the agent's word that damp is "only condensation".
- Not checking the certificates.
- Signing the same day because you were pressured.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: the agent's redress scheme and client money protection, the landlord's gas safety certificate and EICR, the EPC register on GOV.UK, and the council's licensing register for shared houses. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

::: {.next}
- Make a one-page viewing checklist and save it on your phone.
- Book at least three viewings in two days.
- Ask for the safety certificates and EPC in writing for each home you like.
:::

## Applying, referencing and the holding deposit {.chapter number="6"}

This chapter explains what happens after you choose a home: the application, the checks, the holding deposit and how to get through referencing as a newcomer with no UK history. By the end you should be able to apply confidently and keep your holding deposit safe.

### What you need to know

Once you pick a home you apply to the agent or landlord. They check that you can afford the rent, that you can lawfully rent (Chapter 7) and that you have been a reliable tenant. This is called referencing. Newcomers often have no UK references, so understanding what is asked, and what alternatives exist, is the difference between a quick yes and a long wait.

### The application, step by step

1. **Make an offer at the advertised rent.** In England, landlords and agents must not encourage or accept offers above the advertised rent (Chapter 2).
2. **Complete the application form** with accurate information. Giving false information can lose you the home and your holding deposit.
3. **Pay the holding deposit** if one is requested, by bank transfer, and get a receipt that states the amount, the property, the deadline for the decision and the conditions (see below).
4. **Provide your documents:** ID and right-to-rent proof, proof of income or study, bank statements, references and guarantor details (Chapter 7).
5. **Wait for the decision,** within the agreed deadline, normally 15 days unless you agree otherwise [VERIFY]{.v}.
6. **Sign the tenancy** and pay the deposit and first rent (Chapter 8).

### The holding deposit

A holding deposit reserves the home while the landlord checks you. In England it can be no more than one week's rent. It must be refunded, or put towards your first rent or deposit with your agreement, if you are accepted. The landlord can keep it only in limited cases, set out in law, and must give you the reason in writing [VERIFY: current rules and time limits]{.v}.

| Situation | What should happen to the holding deposit |
|---|---|
| The landlord accepts you and you sign | It becomes part of your first rent or deposit, or is refunded. |
| The landlord decides not to rent to you | Refunded in full, normally within seven days. |
| You change your mind and withdraw | The landlord may keep it. |
| You give false or misleading information | The landlord may keep it. |
| You fail the right-to-rent check | The landlord may keep it. |
| The landlord does not meet the decision deadline | Refunded. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from GOV.UK guidance on the Tenant Fees Act 2019, October 2026. Always read the written holding-deposit terms.
:::

::: {.warn}
Pay a holding deposit only after you have seen the home, only to the account of the letting agent company or the named landlord, and only against a written receipt. Never pay a holding deposit in cash, with a gift card or to a personal account that does not match the agreement.
:::

### What referencing checks

| Check | What is looked at | If you have no UK history |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Passport and visa or eVisa | Use your passport and a share code. |
| Right to rent | Whether you can lawfully rent in England | Share code from your UKVI account (Chapter 7). |
| Income and affordability | Salary or savings; many agents use a rule of 30 times the monthly rent in annual income | Employment contract, offer letter, payslips; savings; or a guarantor. |
| Employment | A reference from your employer | An offer letter and a contact at HR; a reference from your previous employer abroad in English. |
| Previous landlord | Whether you paid rent and looked after the home | A letter or email from a landlord abroad, or a character reference. |
| Credit history | A UK credit check | Newcomers often have none. A guarantor or a larger deposit within the legal limit may be accepted. |
| Student status | A university offer or enrolment letter | Many students use a guarantor (Chapter 12). |

::: {.src}
Editorial summary of common agent practice, October 2026. Criteria differ by agent and landlord.
:::

### If your application is declined

You are not entitled to a full explanation, but you can ask which check failed. If it was a credit or income check, you may be able to offer a guarantor, a professional guarantor service, or evidence of savings. If it was right to rent, make sure the share code is correct and has not expired. If you believe you were refused for an unlawful reason (for example, because of your nationality, race, religion, children or benefits), keep the evidence, write to the agent and contact Shelter, Citizens Advice or the council [VERIFY: your options in your nation]{.v}.

### A newcomer's evidence pack

Keep the following together, in PDF, with clear names:

- Passport photo page and the visa or eVisa share code instructions.
- Offer letter or contract, and the last payslips if you have any.
- Bank statements for the last three months, translated if needed.
- A reference from an employer or former landlord.
- A UK or professional guarantor's details.
- A short introduction describing who you are, where you work or study and why you are a safe tenant.

::: {.example title="Passing referencing with no UK history"}
A pharmacist moving from Accra has an offer letter for £38,000, no UK credit history and no UK guarantor. She provides her contract, three months of Ghanaian bank statements, a reference from her previous employer and a professional guarantor service quote. The agent accepts the guarantor service, and she signs a week later. The holding deposit becomes her first month's rent.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Paying a holding deposit before you check that you can pass right to rent and the guarantor requirements.
- Giving incomplete or inconsistent information.
- Not keeping the receipt and the written terms.
- Missing the decision deadline because you were slow to send documents.
- Applying for several homes and paying several holding deposits at once. Only one holding deposit can be taken for the same home at a time.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: the written holding-deposit terms, the GOV.UK guidance on the Tenant Fees Act 2019, the agent's referencing criteria and the guarantor's terms. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

::: {.next}
- Assemble your evidence pack as PDFs.
- Decide your guarantor route (Chapter 7).
- Ask each agent in writing for their referencing criteria and the holding-deposit terms.

## Right to rent, paperwork and guarantors {.chapter number="7"}

This chapter explains the right-to-rent check in England, how to prove your status with a share code, what documents landlords may ask for, and how guarantors work. By the end you should be able to complete the check in minutes and arrange the guarantee you need.

### What you need to know

In England, landlords and agents must check that every adult tenant has the right to rent before the tenancy starts. The check is the same for UK citizens and visa holders, and the rules require landlords to treat everyone consistently. Checks are done in person, by a video call or online, and they are separate from referencing. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland do not currently require the same landlord checks [VERIFY: your nation]{.v}.

::: {.plain title="Right to rent in one line"}
A landlord in England must see proof that you are allowed to live in the UK before you move in, and must keep a copy. Refusing to take part means you cannot rent in England.
:::

### How the check works

| Your situation | How you prove your right to rent |
|---|---|
| British or Irish citizen | A UK or Irish passport, or other accepted documents. |
| Visa holder with an eVisa (most newcomers) | A share code generated from your UKVI account, and your date of birth [VERIFY: current process]{.v}. |
| Pre-settled or settled status | A share code. |
| Student in halls or approved student accommodation | Often exempt from the check, but check the contract and the landlord [VERIFY: current exemptions]{.v}. |
| Lodger living with the landlord | The landlord should still check your right to rent [VERIFY]{.v}. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from GOV.UK right to rent guidance, October 2026.
:::

### Your share code, step by step

1. **Sign in to your UKVI account** with your email and the two-factor code.
2. **Choose "Prove your right to rent"** and generate a share code. It is a nine-character code that starts with R.
3. **Send the share code and your date of birth** to the landlord or agent. They enter them on the Home Office website, see your status and take a copy.
4. **Check the outcome** with the landlord. If the check fails, contact the Home Office service named on the screen.
5. **Keep a record** of the date, the code and the confirmation.

::: {.warn}
A share code is time limited and for one purpose. Give it only to a landlord or agent you have verified. Do not send your passport scan or a screenshot of your whole UKVI account to anyone you have not checked. Never pay to get a share code.
:::

### What documents landlords may ask for

| Document | Why they ask | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Passport and visa or eVisa | Identity and right to rent | Needed for the check. |
| Offer letter or contract | Income | Keep a copy. |
| Last three payslips and bank statements | Affordability | If you are new, show your offer letter and savings. |
| Proof of address | Identity | Your previous address abroad, translated if needed. |
| Employer or landlord reference | Reliability | Ask for it in English. |
| Guarantor details | Credit risk | See below. |

You do not have to give more than is needed. Landlords can ask for ID and proof of income, but they must handle your data lawfully, and a request for an unusual document, a copy of your whole visa history or payment to see a document is a red flag.

![Passport and documents on a desk. A tidy evidence pack gets you a quicker yes.](images/GBR-05-c07-documents.webp)

### Guarantors

A guarantor is a person or company that agrees to pay your rent if you cannot. Landlords use them when you have no UK credit history or your income is below their test.

| Type | What it is | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Personal guarantor | A UK-based person with stable income and credit history, often a relative or friend | Must usually live in the UK and pass a credit check; relatives abroad rarely qualify. Legally responsible for your rent. |
| Professional guarantor service | A company that guarantees your rent for a fee | Not accepted by every landlord; check the fee, the contract and the company's reputation [VERIFY: current providers and fees]{.v}. |
| Rent guarantee through your employer | Some employers or universities offer one | Ask your HR or accommodation team. |
| Larger deposit | Some landlords accept more of a deposit, but not above the legal cap in England | Cannot exceed the cap. |
| Savings evidence | Bank statements showing several months of rent | Accepted by some landlords. |

::: {.insight}
Choose your guarantor route before you start viewing. If you are abroad and need a UK phone number for guarantor checks and landlord calls, make sure the number you give is reachable and works with verification codes. A reachable number prevents a delay at the last step.
:::

### Step by step: prepare your paperwork

1. **Create or access your UKVI account** and test generating a share code.
2. **Scan your passport and key documents** as PDFs with clear names.
3. **Request references** from your employer and your last landlord, in English.
4. **Choose your guarantor route** and collect the details.
5. **Keep copies** of everything you send, with the date and recipient.

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Giving your share code to a stranger or to an unverified website.
- Waiting until you have found a home to try to log in to your UKVI account.
- Sending your full passport and visa history when only a share code is needed.
- Assuming a guarantor abroad will be accepted.
- Paying a "right to rent fee". Landlords and agents cannot charge you for the check in England.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: the GOV.UK pages on proving your right to rent and on the check for landlords, your UKVI account, and any guarantor service's terms. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

::: {.next}
- Log in to your UKVI account and generate a test share code.
- Scan your key documents and name them.
- Decide your guarantor route and ask each agent which they accept.
:::

## The tenancy agreement: read it before you sign {.chapter number="8"}

This chapter explains what a tenancy agreement is, what it must say, which clauses to read slowly and what to do before you sign. By the end you should be able to read an English agreement in an hour and know what you are agreeing to.

### What you need to know

A tenancy agreement is the contract between you and the landlord. It sets the rent, the rules and your rights. In England since 1 May 2026, most new private tenancies are periodic, which means they roll on until you or the landlord ends them lawfully, so many agreements no longer have a fixed end date (Chapter 2). You should still read the document, because the other terms matter.

::: {.plain title="Joint and several liability"}
If you rent with friends on one agreement, each of you is responsible for the whole rent, not just your share. If one person does not pay, the landlord can ask any of you for the full amount. Choose the people you sign with carefully.
:::

### The key terms to check

| Term | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Parties | Your name and the landlord's name and address (a landlord must give a UK address for notices). |
| Property | The exact address and which rooms and parts you can use. |
| Rent | The amount, how often it is paid, the date and how to pay it. |
| Deposit | The amount, the scheme that will protect it and when you will be told. |
| Tenancy type and start date | Periodic or fixed; the date the tenancy begins. |
| What is included | Furniture, appliances, bills, internet, gardening. |
| Repairs | Who reports repairs, to whom and how quickly the landlord will respond. |
| Access | Notice before the landlord enters, normally 24 hours in writing except in an emergency. |
| Ending the tenancy | Your notice (two months in England for a periodic tenancy) and the landlord's grounds. |
| Rules | Guests, pets, smoking, subletting, decorating, noise. |
| Fees | Only allowed fees; none for the check-in or check-out. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from GOV.UK tenancy agreement guidance and the Renters' Rights Act overview, October 2026. Terms differ by nation.
:::

![A contract on a clipboard. Sign only after you have read the agreement and verified who you are dealing with.](images/GBR-05-c08-signing.webp)

### Clauses that deserve a second look

- **Unfair or unlawful clauses.** A clause that takes away a right you have by law is usually not enforceable. Examples: a clause that lets the landlord enter without notice, a ban on all guests or a clause that charges a fee not allowed by law.
- **Rent review clauses.** After 1 May 2026, rent can rise only through the legal process, whatever an older agreement says.
- **Break clauses and early exit charges.** Check what they say and whether they are still valid under the new rules.
- **Cleaning and replacement clauses.** You can be asked to leave the property clean, but not to pay for professional cleaning unless the home was left dirty beyond a reasonable standard.
- **Pets.** Landlords must consider a pet request and cannot refuse unreasonably in England.
- **Subletting and Airbnb.** Usually banned without permission. Do not do it.
- **Liability for damage.** You are responsible for damage you cause, but not for fair wear and tear.

### Before you sign

1. **Read the whole agreement,** including the schedules, in a quiet place. Use a translation tool only for understanding, not as a replacement for the English text.
2. **Compare the terms with the advert and what you were told,** and ask for any change in writing.
3. **Check the documents the landlord must give you:** the Energy Performance Certificate, a current gas safety record, the electrical safety report and the government's "How to Rent" guide [VERIFY: current list]{.v}.
4. **Check the deposit information:** the scheme name and the date by which it will be protected.
5. **Check the people.** Is the landlord's name the same as the name on the account you will pay? Is the agent licensed?
6. **Ask for time.** A fair landlord gives you a day to read and sign.
7. **Take photos or scans** of every page you sign, and ask for a copy signed by both sides.

### Signing safely, including from abroad

You can sign electronically, but only after you have verified the landlord, seen the home or had it checked, and read the document. Use the agent's official e-signature platform, and confirm the platform's link with the agent by phone. Do not sign a document sent as a screenshot by an unknown account, and never pay before you have the signed agreement.

::: {.warn}
If someone says the agreement can be signed "after you pay", or the landlord is "abroad" and will send the keys by courier, stop. That is the classic advance-fee scam pattern (Chapter 9).
:::

::: {.example title="The clause that was not allowed"}
A tenant in Manchester reads in her draft agreement: "The landlord may enter at any time to inspect." She writes to the agent: "I understand the landlord needs access, but the law requires reasonable notice, normally 24 hours in writing. Please amend the clause." The agent changes it to "on 24 hours' written notice, except in an emergency", and she signs.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Before I sign"}
- I have read every page and every schedule.
- The rent, deposit, bills and dates match the advert and the agent's messages.
- I have the safety certificates, EPC and the government's guide.
- I know which deposit scheme will hold my deposit.
- The landlord's name matches the name on the bank account.
- Everyone who signs knows they may each be liable for the whole rent.
- I have a copy of the signed agreement.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Signing without reading because of a deadline.
- Assuming the agreement can override the law.
- Signing a joint agreement with someone you do not know well.
- Accepting a verbal promise that is not in writing.
- Paying before you get the signed document.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: the GOV.UK tenancy agreement and Renters' Rights Act pages, the documents the landlord must provide, and the deposit scheme details. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::
