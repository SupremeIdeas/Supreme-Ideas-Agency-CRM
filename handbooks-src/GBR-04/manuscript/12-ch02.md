## Choosing a course and applying {.chapter number="2"}

This chapter covers how to choose a course and university, how to apply for an undergraduate or postgraduate place, how to find funding, and how to avoid fake offers and dishonest agents. By the end you should have a short list of courses and a plan with dates.

### What you need to know

**Start by checking the university is real and licensed.** Only licensed sponsors can issue a CAS. Check each university or college on the official GOV.UK register of student sponsors [VERIFY: current register link]{.v}, and for universities in England check the Office for Students register as well [VERIFY]{.v}. If a college is not on the register, do not pay it anything.

**Choose by fit, not by speed.** Pick a course that follows from your earlier study or work. A clear link makes the application stronger and makes the later visa interview or credibility check easier. Look at the course content, the assessment method, the location and what graduates go on to do. Check the full cost, including whether the course includes a work placement.

### What to compare when you choose a university

League tables are a starting point, not an answer. A university that ranks lower overall can be excellent for your subject, cheaper to live in, and kinder to your budget. Compare these seven things for each shortlisted course:

1. **Course content and assessment.** Read the module list. How much is exams, coursework, group work and the dissertation? Does it suit how you learn?
2. **Fees and what they cover.** The exact international fee, whether it can rise each year, and whether lab, fieldwork or placement costs are extra.
3. **Teaching and outcomes.** Student satisfaction and graduate outcomes are published for most UK universities, and England's Teaching Excellence Framework rating gives a quick quality signal [VERIFY: current TEF ratings page]{.v}.
4. **Accommodation guarantee.** Many universities guarantee a room in halls to international first-years who apply on time. That removes your biggest arrival risk (Chapter 9).
5. **Location and cost of living.** A cheaper city can save thousands over a year (Chapter 1).
6. **Support for international students.** Airport pickup, orientation, an international advice team, English support, faith spaces and active African and Caribbean societies.
7. **Career links.** Placement years, employer partnerships, a careers service open to alumni and a track record in your target industry.

### Applying for an undergraduate course

Undergraduate applications go through UCAS, the central admissions service. You can apply to up to five courses. UCAS sets deadlines each year, and the main deadline for most courses is in January. Some courses, including medicine, dentistry, veterinary science and Oxford and Cambridge, have an earlier deadline in October [VERIFY: UCAS deadlines for your entry year]{.v}.

1.  **Choose up to five courses** that fit your grades and interests.

2.  **Check entry requirements for your qualifications.** Each university publishes what it accepts from West African exams and other systems [VERIFY: the university's page for your country]{.v}. Some require a foundation year or an international foundation programme.

3.  **Write your personal statement,** in your own words. Universities use checks to detect copied or generated text, and a weak or copied statement can end an application.

4.  **Get your reference and documents ready,** including transcripts and certificates.

5.  **Submit before the deadline,** then watch your UCAS account and email for offers.

6.  **Reply to offers** by the dates UCAS gives. Offers can be conditional (you must meet grades or other conditions) or unconditional.

### Applying for a postgraduate course

Most taught master's applications go directly to the university through its own online portal. There is usually no single national deadline. Many universities admit on a rolling basis and close courses when they are full. Applying six to nine months before the start date gives you time for the offer, the funds, the English test and the visa.

1.  **Shortlist three to five courses** and check each course page for fees, entry requirements and deadlines.

2.  **Prepare your documents:** degree certificate and transcripts, CV, personal statement, references, passport and English evidence if you already have it.

3.  **Apply through the university's portal.** Use one email address and keep a copy of every submission.

4.  **Respond to the offer.** The university may ask for a deposit to hold your place. Check whether it is refundable if your visa is refused, and get that in writing.

5.  **Meet the conditions,** such as your final transcript or English test result.

6.  **Wait for your CAS.** The university issues it after you meet the conditions and, where it asks for one, pay the deposit. Chapter 3 explains what to do with it.

### Pathways: foundation years and pre-sessional English

If your grades or English are just short of the offer, a pathway can turn a "no" into a "yes".

- **International foundation year.** A one-year course at or linked to the university that prepares you for a named degree. Check that progression is guaranteed if you pass, and that the foundation provider is a licensed sponsor.
- **Pre-master's course.** A bridging course for students whose first degree does not quite fit a master's.
- **Pre-sessional English.** An intensive English course, usually 6 to 12 weeks before the main course starts, that can replace an English test result for that university [VERIFY: the Home Office English rule for the Student visa still requires approved evidence; ask whether the course can be taken within the university's own English assessment]{.v}. Ask your university how this interacts with your visa.

Use only the official scholarship website, and never pay to apply for a scholarship. Genuine scholarships do not ask you for an application fee, and scholarship scams often use the names Chevening or Commonwealth.

### Funding beyond the big names

The big scholarships are very competitive. A smart application plan uses several layers of funding, because partial awards add up.

- **University scholarships and bursaries.** Often automatic for strong applicants or applied for at the same time as the course. Look for "international scholarships" on each university's fees page.
- **Country and regional awards.** Many universities set aside awards for specific countries or regions, and some for African students [VERIFY: each university's current list]{.v}.
- **Foundation and charity awards.** For example, programmes funded by the Mastercard Foundation at selected UK universities, the Rhodes Scholarship at Oxford and the Gates Cambridge Scholarship for postgraduates [VERIFY: eligibility, countries and deadlines for each]{.v}.
- **Employer or government sponsorship.** Some employers and governments pay for study if you return to work for them. Read the bond terms carefully.
- **Fee payment plans.** Some universities let you pay tuition in instalments. That helps your cash flow, but remember the Home Office still wants the first year's tuition shown (or already paid) when you apply for your visa.

::: {.warn}
A "scholarship" that asks for a fee, a personal bank transfer, your passwords, or that arrives by WhatsApp or text from an unknown number is a scam. Official scholarships never charge to apply. Check the scholarship's own website, typed into your browser, before replying.
:::

### Using an agent safely

Many universities work with education agents, and for authorised agents it is usually the university that pays the agent, not you. Agents can be genuinely helpful with documents and deadlines. The problem is the unauthorised ones, and the ones who make promises.

| **Normal and acceptable**                                           | **Warning signs**                                                                 |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| The agent is listed on the university's own website as authorised. | The agent is not on the university's list, or the university does not know them. |
| The agent helps you complete forms and check documents.             | The agent offers to write your statement or change your documents for you.        |
| Fees are explained in writing before you pay.                       | The agent asks for cash or payment to a personal account.                         |
| You keep control of your own application accounts.                  | The agent wants your passwords or applies from their own email address.           |
| The agent says admission and visa decisions are made by others.     | The agent guarantees admission, a visa, a scholarship or a job.                   |

::: src
Compiled from UKCISA and university guidance on agents and fraud, October 2026.
:::

::: {.plain title="Fake offers and fake cas"}
A fake offer letter or fake CAS can look real. Real documents come from the university itself, through its official system. A CAS is an electronic record with a reference number that the university can confirm. If a university says it did not issue the document, it is fake, and using it can lead to a refusal and a ban of up to ten years [VERIFY: current ban length for false representations]{.v}.
:::

### Working with an agent: what the rules say

An education agent who recruits students for a university is paid by the university and is not allowed to charge you hidden fees for the same service. But there is a second, separate rule that catches students out: **only people with the right permission can give immigration advice.** In the UK that means a solicitor, a barrister or an adviser registered with the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner (OISC) [VERIFY: current rule and register link]{.v}. An agent who tells you what to put on your visa form, or which "route" to use for the best chance, may be giving regulated advice. Ask who regulates them and check the register yourself.

### Step by step: build your application plan

1.  **Make a short list of three to five courses** that are real, licensed and affordable.

2.  **Write the dates** for each application, offer reply, deposit, English test, funds period and visa application on one calendar.

3.  **Check each university's refund terms** for deposits and for visa refusal.

4.  **Apply early,** especially if you need a scholarship.

5.  **Keep copies** of every submission, email and payment receipt in one folder.

::: {.example title="Checking an offer before paying a deposit"}
A student receives an email with an offer and a request for a £5,000 deposit to a named account. He does not click the link. He logs in to the university's website directly, finds his application, and sees no offer. He calls the university's international office using the number on its website. The office confirms the email did not come from them. He reports the email, and pays nothing.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Before i accept an offer or pay a deposit"}
-   The offer appears in my account on the university's own portal.
-   The university is on the official sponsor register.
-   The deposit goes to the university's own bank account, shown on its official website.
-   I have the refund terms in writing, including if my visa is refused.
-   I have the exact tuition fee and the funds I must show.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

-   Paying a deposit to an account given in an email without confirming it with the university.

-   Applying too late for scholarships. Many close a year before the course starts.

-   Letting an agent write the personal statement. Universities can detect it, and it can lead to rejection.

-   Choosing a college that is not on the official sponsor register.

-   Accepting an offer without reading the conditions, then missing a deadline for the English test or transcript.
