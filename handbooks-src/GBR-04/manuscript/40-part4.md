# University life {.part number="IV" sub="How UK teaching and assessment really work, how to protect your academic record, where to find help, and how to turn your degree into a career."}

## How teaching and assessment work {.chapter number="12"}

This chapter explains how UK courses are taught and marked, what is expected of you outside the classroom, and how to get good marks without burning out. By the end you should be able to read a module handbook, plan a week of study, and ask for feedback in the way UK tutors expect.

### What you need to know

UK higher education assumes that you are an independent adult learner. You will have fewer timetabled hours than you may be used to, and a lot of unsupervised time in which you are expected to read, think, write and practise. The teaching is the starting point of your learning, not the whole of it.

| Format | What happens | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| Lecture | A large-group presentation of the main ideas, often recorded | Take notes, then read around the topic the same week. |
| Seminar or tutorial | A small discussion group led by a tutor | Prepare the reading and bring one question or opinion. |
| Lab or practical | Hands-on work with equipment or software | Read the safety notes, prepare the method, write up promptly. |
| Workshop or studio | Project-based work in creative, design or engineering subjects | Bring drafts and be ready to give and receive critique. |
| Supervision | One-to-one or small-group meetings for a dissertation or project | Send an agenda, show progress and record the actions. |
| Placement or fieldwork | Work-based or field-based learning | Treat it as a job, with its own rules for attendance and conduct. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from university course handbooks, October 2026. Formats differ by subject.
:::

![A lecturer at a lectern. Most universities record lectures, but attending and joining the discussion is how you learn the culture of the subject.](images/GBR-04-c12-lecture.webp)

### How much time to study

A full-time degree is a full-time job. A common planning rule is about 35 to 40 hours a week of combined teaching and independent study in term time. At undergraduate level there may be 10 to 15 timetabled hours, and the rest is reading, writing, group work and revision. At master's level there can be fewer timetabled hours but a heavier reading load, and a dissertation of 10,000 to 15,000 words that takes the whole summer.

### Assessment: how marks are earned

Each module has a "module handbook" that tells you the learning outcomes, the assessment methods, the weighting of each piece of work, the submission dates and the marking criteria. Read it in week one and put every deadline in your calendar.

| Assessment type | What it tests | Tips |
|---|---|---|
| Essay or report | Argument, evidence and writing | Answer the question set, reference properly, and use the marking criteria as a checklist. |
| Exam (seen or unseen, closed or open book) | Recall and application under time pressure | Practise past papers, and plan your time per question. |
| Presentation | Communication and understanding | Rehearse to time and prepare for questions. |
| Group project | Teamwork and delivery | Agree roles early and keep a record of your contribution. |
| Dissertation or project | Independent research | Meet your supervisor early and often. |
| Practical or portfolio | Skills in action | Keep dated records and reflect on what you learned. |

### Marks, feedback and what a "good" mark means

In the UK, marks are lower than in many education systems, and that is normal. A mark of 70 or more is a first-class mark and is rare. A mark in the 60s is a strong result, and 50 to 59 is a solid pass. Read the feedback closely, because it explains what the marker rewarded and what lost marks, and use it in your next piece of work. If you do not understand your feedback, go to your tutor's feedback hours.

::: {.plain title="Credit, pass marks and resits"}
Each module carries credits. You pass a module by reaching the pass mark, usually 40 at undergraduate level and 50 at master's level [VERIFY: your university's regulations]{.v}. If you fail, you are usually allowed a resit, sometimes capped at the pass mark. Resits and repeated years have visa and fee consequences, so talk to your tutor and the international advice team early.
:::

### When life gets in the way

Illness, bereavement, a family emergency or a technical failure can affect your work. Universities have a process for this, often called mitigating or extenuating circumstances. You normally need to submit a form and evidence **before or very soon after** the deadline. Waiting until after a result is released is usually too late. If you need to pause your studies, ask your international advice team about the visa effect before you submit any form.

### Habits that work for international students

1. **Go to the first week's induction and read the module handbooks.**
2. **Plan your week on Sunday,** with study blocks in your calendar.
3. **Start assignments early,** with a draft at least a week before the deadline.
4. **Use academic skills services,** writing centres and library workshops, which are free and made for you.
5. **Join a study group** to explain ideas aloud and to hear other approaches.
6. **Ask questions** in seminars, even if your English feels slow. Tutors value contribution.

::: {.example title="Turning feedback into a better mark"}
A master's student receives 58 for his first essay. The feedback says that his description is good but his argument is thin and his references are uneven. He books a 20-minute writing-centre session and brings the essay and the marking criteria. He learns to state a claim in each paragraph, support it with two sources and link it to the question. His next essay scores 66.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Treating the first term as a settling-in period and starting work late.
- Reading only the lecture slides.
- Missing the deadline for extenuating circumstances.
- Not asking for feedback.
- Underestimating how long a dissertation takes.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your module handbooks, your university's assessment and resit regulations, and the policy on mitigating circumstances. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

- Block 20 hours a week of independent study.

:::

## Academic integrity, referencing and AI {.chapter number="13"}

This chapter explains what UK universities mean by academic misconduct, how to reference, what is allowed with AI tools, and what happens if you are accused. By the end you should be able to protect your degree and your visa from the most avoidable risk of student life.

### What you need to know

UK universities take academic integrity very seriously. A finding of misconduct can mean a zero for the work, a fail for the module, a repeated year or expulsion. Expulsion also ends your sponsorship, and the university must report it to the Home Office, which can cancel your visa. That is why the most important study skill for an international student is to understand the rules **before** the first submission.

::: {.plain title="The core idea"}
Your work must be your own, and every idea, quotation, figure or sentence that is not yours must be shown as someone else's through a reference. You may use sources, and you are expected to. You may not pass them off as your own.
:::

### What counts as misconduct

| Behaviour | Example | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Plagiarism | Copying text or ideas without a reference, or rewording a source too closely | Penalty from a mark reduction to a fail, depending on the extent. |
| Self-plagiarism | Reusing your own earlier work without permission | Treated like plagiarism. |
| Collusion | Working with others on individual work | Penalty for everyone involved. |
| Contract cheating | Paying someone or a service to write or complete work | Serious penalties; commercial essay-writing services are illegal to provide or advertise in England. |
| Fabrication or falsification | Inventing data, sources or results | Serious, and can be treated as research misconduct. |
| Cheating in an exam | Notes, phones, copying or impersonation | Usually the most severe penalties. |
| Using AI in a way that is not allowed | Submitting AI-written text as your own | Treated as misconduct where the rules forbid it. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from university academic-integrity policies and England's Skills and Post-16 Education Act 2022, October 2026. Penalties are set by each university.
:::

### Using AI tools: ask, declare, keep your voice

Rules about generative AI differ between universities, schools and even modules. Some allow it for brainstorming, language checking or coding help, if you declare it. Others forbid it for assessed work. Four habits keep you safe.

1. **Read the policy for each module** and, if it is unclear, ask the tutor in writing.
2. **Declare any use,** using the university's wording, when it is allowed.
3. **Keep your drafts and notes,** so you can show how your work developed.
4. **Never submit AI-generated text as your own** where it is not allowed. Detection tools exist, and tutors also notice a sudden change in voice or detail.

::: {.warn}
A message or advert offering to "write your assignment" or "guarantee a grade" is contract cheating. These services also blackmail students, threatening to report them unless they pay again. If it happens, tell your university's student services. They treat it as a safeguarding matter.
:::

![Studying with a laptop and books. Reference as you read, not at the end.](images/GBR-04-c13-study-desk.webp)

### How to reference

A reference has two parts: a short note in the text that shows where an idea comes from, and a full entry in a list at the end. Your department tells you which style to use. The most common are Harvard (author and year), APA (also author and date), OSCOLA (footnotes, used in law) and numeric styles (used in sciences and engineering). Use a reference manager, such as Zotero or Mendeley, from week one, and keep a note of every source as you read.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Put quotations in quotation marks and cite them | Paste text from a website and change a few words |
| Paraphrase in your own words and still cite | Copy a diagram without credit |
| Use the style your department asks for | Mix styles in one piece of work |
| Keep a list of sources as you go | Add references at the end from memory |

### If you are accused of misconduct

1. **Do not panic and do not ignore the email.** Respond by the date given.
2. **Read the notice.** It tells you what is alleged and what evidence there is.
3. **Ask for help.** The students' union advice service and the international advice team can attend meetings with you and help you prepare.
4. **Tell the truth.** Explain what happened, show your drafts and notes, and accept responsibility where you made a mistake.
5. **Ask about the visa effect** before you accept a penalty such as a repeated year.
6. **Know your right to appeal.** If you remain unhappy after the university's process, you can complain to the Office of the Independent Adjudicator for Higher Education (in England and Wales), the Scottish Public Services Ombudsman, or the Northern Ireland Public Services Ombudsman [VERIFY: complaint routes and time limits]{.v}.

::: {.example title="A reference habit that saved a degree"}
A student is called to a misconduct meeting after her essay scores 41 per cent in a plagiarism check. She brings her notes, her reading list and her drafts. The panel sees that she paraphrased in her own words but missed a few citations. She receives a reduced mark and a warning, not a fail, and she learns to reference as she writes.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Assuming that rules from your home university apply in the UK.
- Using AI without checking the module's rules.
- Paying a "proof-reading" service that rewrites your work.
- Sharing your coursework with friends before it is marked.
- Ignoring a misconduct email.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your university's academic integrity policy, the module's AI guidance, your department's referencing style guide, and the Office of the Independent Adjudicator or the equivalent ombudsman for your nation. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

## Support, wellbeing and community {.chapter number="14"}

This chapter explains the free support you can get as an international student, how to look after your mental and physical health, and how to build a community. By the end you should know who to ask for what, and what to do in an emergency.

### What you need to know

Studying abroad is rewarding and hard. Many students feel isolated, anxious or overwhelmed at some point. This is a normal response to a big change, and UK universities have teams whose job is to help you. Using them is not a sign of weakness. It is a sign of good planning.

| Who | Helps with | How to find them |
|---|---|---|
| Personal tutor or academic adviser | Your progress, choices and referrals | Named in your course handbook or portal. |
| International advice team | Visas, work rules, travel, money and compliance | The international office or welcome team. Free and confidential. |
| Student wellbeing and mental health service | Counselling, short-term therapy, workshops | Self-referral through the student services website. |
| Disability and inclusion team | Adjustments for health, learning difficulties or disability | Register early, and bring evidence. |
| Students' union advice service | Academic appeals, housing, misconduct and complaints | Independent of the university and free. |
| Chaplaincy and faith spaces | Prayer, worship, pastoral care | Often open to all faiths. |
| Careers service | CVs, interviews, placements and employers | Open to you during your course and often after you graduate. |
| University security and campus safety | Emergencies and safe-travel services | Numbers on the back of your student card. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from university student-services guides, October 2026.
:::


### Mental health: knowing the signs and the numbers

Common signs of strain include poor sleep, constant worry, losing interest in food or friends, and feeling hopeless. If these last for more than two weeks, or you feel unsafe, ask for help.

::: {.warn}
In an emergency, call **999**. For urgent help with your mental health in England, call **NHS 111** and choose the mental health option [VERIFY: current NHS 111 mental health option]{.v}. The Samaritans are available free at any time on **116 123**. You can also text **SHOUT** to **85258** for free, confidential support. Keep these numbers in your phone.
:::

Your university's wellbeing service can usually see you within days or weeks, and many offer drop-in sessions and group workshops. Help is confidential, and using it does not affect your visa. A counsellor will not report you to the Home Office for feeling unwell.

### Building a community

- **Join a society in the first fortnight.** There are societies for hobbies, faith, sport, subjects and countries, including African and Caribbean societies in many universities.
- **Make friends beyond your national group.** A mixed circle improves your English, your confidence and your network.
- **Say yes to one event a week.** A meal, a walk, a game or a volunteering session.
- **Find your faith or cultural community** if that matters to you. Places of worship and cultural groups are often generous to students.
- **Keep in touch with home** at set times, but do not let it replace local life.

### Discrimination, racism and harassment

UK law (the Equality Act 2010) protects you from discrimination because of race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, disability, age and other protected characteristics. Universities in England must also have rules against harassment and sexual misconduct, and must tell you how to report it [VERIFY: current regulatory requirements]{.v}. If you experience racism or harassment, write down what happened, when and who was involved, then report it to the university's reporting service, your students' union or the police for crimes. You do not have to put up with it, and reporting it does not put your visa at risk.

### Taking care of your body

- **Register with a GP** in your first week (Chapter 18).
- **Eat regularly** and cook with others when you can.
- **Move:** walking, sport, dancing, the gym. Many universities offer cheap sport memberships.
- **Sleep:** keep a routine, especially in the first term.
- **Take care with alcohol and drugs.** UK drug laws are strict, and a conviction can end your visa.

### Step by step: build your support map

1. **Write the names and numbers** of your personal tutor, the international advice team, the wellbeing service and campus security.
2. **Register with the disability and inclusion team** if you need adjustments.
3. **Save the emergency numbers** in your phone.
4. **Choose one society and one regular activity.**
5. **Tell one person** how you are doing every week.

::: {.example title="Asking early"}
A student feels anxious about exams and has trouble sleeping. In week four he books a drop-in session with the wellbeing service. A counsellor teaches him breathing and planning techniques, and the tutor agrees to a short extension for one piece of work, with evidence. By the exam period he is sleeping better and has a revision plan.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Waiting until the exam period to ask for help.
- Assuming that counselling will affect your visa. It will not.
- Isolating yourself because of language or culture.
- Ignoring discrimination or harassment.
- Not registering with disability support early enough for adjustments.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your university's student services website for current contact details and opening hours, and the NHS pages for mental health services in your nation. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

- Save the wellbeing service and emergency numbers in your phone.
- Join one society this fortnight.
- Book an introductory chat with your personal tutor.

## Attendance, compliance and your career from day one {.chapter number="15"}

This chapter joins two things that international students often treat separately: staying on the right side of your visa conditions, and building the experience that will turn your degree into a job. By the end you should know what your university reports, how to use placements and internships lawfully, and how to start a UK-style career plan.

### What you need to know

Your university is a licensed sponsor, and its licence depends on you following your visa conditions. That is why it monitors attendance and enrolment, and why it must tell the Home Office about certain events. At the same time, UK employers value placements, projects and professional networks as much as your marks. The good news is that following the rules and building your career can go together.

### What your university has to report

Sponsors must report certain events to the Home Office, usually through a government system. The main ones are in the table.

| Event | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| You do not enrol on time | The university reports you, and your visa may be curtailed. |
| You stop attending or miss a run of required contact points | The university must report students who stop engaging with the course [VERIFY: current threshold for missed contact points]{.v}. |
| You change course, level or university | A new CAS and visa application are usually needed. |
| You suspend or intermit your studies | Your permission to stay is usually affected; seek advice before you agree. |
| You leave early or complete early | Your visa may be shortened. |
| You are found to have committed serious misconduct | Your sponsorship may end. |
| Your contact details change | You must update them with the university. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from Home Office sponsor guidance and university international-office pages, October 2026.
:::

::: {.plain title="Attendance is a visa matter"}
Missing lectures is not only an academic risk. It can become a visa risk. If you are ill or have an emergency, tell the university quickly, in writing, and keep evidence. A short, early message protects you far better than silence.
:::

### Working lawfully while you study

Chapter 17 covers part-time jobs. Placements and internships need extra care.

- **Course placements.** A placement that is part of your course and arranged through the university is usually allowed, but check how the hours count against your work limit [VERIFY: how placement hours are treated for your visa]{.v}.
- **Voluntary work.** Genuine volunteering for a charity is generally allowed. A "volunteer" role that is really an unpaid job for a business is risky [VERIFY: guidance on volunteering and unpaid work]{.v}.
- **Paid internships.** They count as work and must stay within your weekly hours in term time, and full-time in official vacations.
- **Self-employment and freelancing.** Not allowed on the Student visa. Do not sell services, run a business or take freelance clients.

### Building a career in the UK

1. **Visit the careers service in your first month.** They offer CV checks, mock interviews and employer events.
2. **Write a UK-style CV.** Two pages, with a short profile, work and education in reverse order, achievements with numbers, and no photograph or date of birth.
3. **Create a LinkedIn profile** and connect with classmates, lecturers and alumni.
4. **Apply for placements and graduate schemes early.** Many close in the autumn for the following year.
5. **Join a society or project** that gives you responsibilities you can talk about.
6. **Learn the visa rules for jobs.** Many employers need to sponsor you, and only some can (Chapter 20).
7. **Keep a "proof" folder** of projects, references and results, so you can show what you did.

![A careers fair. Employers use these events to meet students, so bring questions about sponsorship and international-friendly roles.](images/GBR-04-c15-careers-fair.webp)

### Study abroad, placements and exchange years

Some degrees offer a placement year or a term abroad. Check how a year out affects your visa and fees. A placement year may need a new CAS, and a year abroad may mean you are not in the UK, with consequences for your permission. Always ask the international advice team before you commit.

::: {.example title="A placement that worked"}
A student on a four-year engineering course takes a paid placement year with a manufacturer. The university checks his visa and issues a new CAS for the longer course. He works 40 hours a week in the placement because it is part of his course, and his visa conditions allow it. The company offers him a graduate job, but cannot sponsor a visa, so he uses the Graduate Route after finishing (Chapter 20) and looks for a sponsor during that time.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Compliance and career checklist"}
- I know the dates for enrolment, attendance and reporting.
- I have told the university my UK address and phone number.
- I know my weekly work limit and my vacation dates.
- I will ask the international advice team before changing course, intermitting or taking a placement.
- I have booked a careers service appointment.
- My CV is in UK style and checked by someone else.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Missing classes without telling the university.
- Taking a job or placement that breaks your work conditions.
- Starting job applications in your final term instead of your first.
- Using an unpaid "internship" that is really a job.
- Assuming that any employer can sponsor your visa.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your university's attendance policy, the GOV.UK Student visa work conditions, and the careers service's guidance on placements. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::
