# Preparing to leave {.part number="II" sub="Documents and funds, tests and medicals, travel, staying connected and a budget you can live with. Start this part the day you accept your offer."}

## Documents and your 28-day money plan {.chapter number="4"}

This chapter covers the paperwork a Student visa application is built on, and how to plan the money so that the 28-day rule never catches you out. By the end you should have a document folder, a dated money plan and a list of people who need to sign things for you.

### What you need to know

A Student visa application is a bundle of evidence. Every item has to be in the right form, say the right thing and agree with every other item. Most refusals for "funds" are not about a lack of money. They are about dates, account owners and missing proof, which are all things you can fix months before you apply.

Start from the end. Choose the date you will submit the application (no earlier than six months before your course starts, and after your CAS, English test, TB certificate and any ATAS certificate are in your hand). Count back 28 days from the date the funds period must end, which must itself be within 31 days before you apply. That gives you the first day on which the full amount must already be in the account.

::: {.insight}
Build the money plan on a calendar before you send any money to the university. Many families move savings between accounts, pay a deposit and top up the balance in the same weeks. Each move can break the 28-day rule by a day or two. Decide who moves what, and when, then write the dates down.
:::

### The document folder

Keep originals in one folder at home, and digital scans in a second folder you can open on your phone. Name every file clearly, for example *passport-bio-page.pdf* or *bank-statement-jan-2027.pdf*, so you can upload fast and never mistake one for another.

| Document | Why it matters | Common problem |
|---|---|---|
| Passport (valid for the whole course and ideally beyond) | Identity and the travel document your eVisa is linked to | Passport expires during the course, forcing a new application. |
| CAS (Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies) | The reference number is the core of your application | Name, date of birth or passport number does not match the passport. |
| Bank statements or other financial evidence | Proof of the tuition and living-cost funds | Wrong account owner, a gap in the 28 days, or a statement that is too old. |
| Consent letter and proof of relationship (if a parent or guardian provides the funds) | Shows the money is lawfully available to you | Letter has no signature, date or account number; birth certificate not translated. |
| English language evidence | Shows you meet the B2 requirement | Test is too old, from a provider that is not approved, or from the wrong test type. |
| TB test certificate (if required) | Public-health requirement for listed countries | Clinic is not approved, or the certificate has expired. |
| ATAS certificate (if required) | Security clearance for sensitive subjects | Applied for too late; ATAS takes at least 30 working days. |
| Academic documents (degree certificate, transcripts) | Support the course and the genuine-student check | Not translated, or inconsistent with the CAS. |
| Passport photographs and identity check | Your application needs an image and an identity check | Photo does not meet the guidance. |

:::

### Whose money can you use?

Funds can come from **your own account**, or from the **account of your parents or legal guardians** with the right proof. In the second case you normally need the original birth certificate that shows the relationship, and a signed letter from the parent confirming that the money is available for your studies [VERIFY: current list of accepted documents and who counts as a "legal guardian"]{.v}. A sibling, uncle, employer or friend is not enough for this route. They can pay your tuition directly to the university, but then the university's receipt, not their bank statement, is your evidence.

Other sources the Home Office accepts include an official financial sponsor (such as a government or the British Council) and some regulated education loans in your own name, with strict conditions on dates and wording [VERIFY: loan evidence rules]{.v}. Savings held in shares, bonds, crypto assets, business accounts and mobile-money wallets are generally not accepted. Money must be in cleared funds at a bank or building society on the Home Office's accepted list.

::: {.warn}
Never lend or borrow money "for the visa" and return it afterwards. It is the most common reason for a refusal and a ban, and sponsors, banks and the Home Office can verify account histories. If you only have part of the money, ask the university about a lower-cost course, a payment plan, a scholarship, or applying later.
:::

### Converting naira, cedis, shillings and rand

The Home Office compares your funds to the pounds required. If your statements are in naira, cedis, Kenyan shillings or another currency, you must show that the amount converted into sterling meets the requirement, using a rate from a recognised source on the date of your application [VERIFY: the official converter or rate source the Home Office accepts]{.v}. Currency swings can change your balance in pounds by thousands between the day you plan and the day you apply. Add a safety margin of at least 5 to 10 per cent in a volatile currency.

| Currency risk | What to do |
|---|---|
| The naira or cedi may fall while your 28 days run | Keep a buffer above the required amount, not exactly on it. |
| A bank may block or delay a large transfer | Keep the money in the same account, or move it long before the 28-day period starts. |
| Exchange-control rules may limit foreign transfers | Pay tuition through your bank's recognised international payment service and keep the receipt. |

### Step by step: build your money and document plan

1. **Write down the exact amount** required: first-year tuition (minus anything the university has already received) plus living costs for your course length up to nine months, using the monthly rate for your application date (Chapter 3).
2. **Choose your application date** and count back to the first day of the 28-day period.
3. **Decide whose account holds the money** and make sure that person is the account owner and can provide a consent letter and the original birth certificate.
4. **Ask the bank for statements** that show the account holder's name, the account number, the bank's name and address, the date of each statement and the balance each day (or on enough dates to show 28 days). Check the bank is on the accepted list.
5. **Translate any document** that is not in English or Welsh, using a translator who certifies the translation with their name, signature, date and contact details [VERIFY: certification wording the Home Office requires]{.v}.
6. **Scan everything** and name the files. Keep the originals safe.
7. **Do a dry run.** Ask someone you trust to read the CAS and the bank statements and to find any date or name mismatch before you apply.

::: {.example title="Counting back 28 days"}
A student plans to start a course on 15 September. She will apply on 10 July, which is more than six weeks before the course. Her funds must be held for 28 days that end no earlier than 9 June (31 days before 10 July). She decides that her father will move the money on 25 April, so the balance is in place from 26 April, long before the first possible end date. She asks for statements on 30 May and again on 8 July. Her application is clean, and no amount of currency movement in the last week can trigger a problem.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Is my money and document plan ready?"}
- I know the exact amount and the monthly rate for my application date.
- The account owner is me, or my parent or legal guardian, with the right proof.
- The 28 days are covered, and I have a buffer for currency changes.
- My statements show the account holder's name, account number, balance and dates.
- All non-English documents have certified translations.
- My passport is valid for the whole course.
- Everything is scanned, named and backed up.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Moving money between accounts during the 28-day period.
- Using an uncle's, sibling's or employer's account without a lawful route.
- Showing a statement that is older than the rules allow.
- Forgetting that the university tuition deposit reduces the tuition you must show, and recording it wrongly.
- Not allowing time for translations and certified copies.
- Planning on today's exchange rate with no buffer.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: GOV.UK Student visa money and documents pages, your university's financial-evidence checklist and the list of banks accepted for visa evidence. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

- Write your application date and count back to day one of the 28-day period.
- Ask the account owner for statements and for a signed consent letter that matches the university's template.
- Start translations now if any document is not in English.
:::

## English tests, medicals and other approvals {.chapter number="5"}

This chapter covers the evidence the Home Office wants beyond money: proof of your English, a TB certificate, an ATAS certificate where your course needs one, vaccinations and the clearance checks that apply to some subjects. By the end you should know which of these apply to you, in what order to do them, and how long each takes.

### What you need to know

Four checks catch students out because they run on other people's timetables: the English test centre, the TB clinic, the ATAS office and the vaccination clinic. None of them is expensive compared to the cost of a failed application, and all of them can be done weeks ahead.

::: {.plain title="The order that works"}
First confirm what your university will accept for English. Then book the English test. While you wait for results, ask the admissions office whether your course needs ATAS. Book your TB test about two to three months before you plan to apply, because certificates are valid for a limited time. Finally, check vaccinations and your health records before you travel.
:::

### English evidence

For a degree-level course, you need English at CEFR level B2. You show it in one of four ways: a Secure English Language Test (SELT) from an approved provider; a degree taught in English from an approved institution (with an Ecctis assessment); a qualifying UK school qualification; or nationality or a degree from an exempt country. Some universities assess English themselves, but the test must still be equivalent to B2, and the Home Office rules apply [VERIFY: current approved tests and whether your university can assess English itself]{.v}.

| Test | What it is | Planning notes |
|---|---|---|
| IELTS for UKVI | The best-known SELT | Offered on paper and computer; results usually within days to two weeks. |
| PTE Academic UKVI | Computer-based | Fast results, often within a few days. |
| LanguageCert Academic SELT | Computer-based | Offered at approved centres. |
| Trinity College London SELT | Face-to-face speaking and listening | Limited centres in some countries. |

::: {.src}
Approved SELT providers and scores are set by the Home Office; confirm the current list on GOV.UK. Fees vary by country.
:::

A university may ask for a higher score than the Home Office does, and for minimum scores in each skill (speaking, listening, reading and writing). Check both. A test taken **too early** may expire before you apply. Most SELT results are valid for two years [VERIFY: validity]{.v}.


### TB test

Applicants from the countries on the Home Office list who will stay in the UK for more than six months need a TB certificate from an approved clinic. For most African countries, the clinic lists are on GOV.UK. You will need your passport and photographs, and the clinic will take a chest X-ray. The certificate shows a clear result; if the X-ray shows something that needs follow-up, you may need further tests and the process takes longer, so do not leave it to the last minute [VERIFY: certificate validity, process for a repeat test]{.v}.

### ATAS, vaccinations and health records

**ATAS.** A free certificate from the UK government for students in some sensitive subjects; at least 30 working days (Chapter 3). **Vaccinations.** The UK does not require specific vaccinations for the Student visa, but the NHS advises students to be up to date with measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) and to have the MenACWY vaccine before or when they start university [VERIFY: current NHS advice]{.v}. Meningitis and measles spread quickly in halls. Bring your vaccination records. **Health records and medicines.** Bring a short summary from your doctor of any long-term condition, and enough of a prescribed medicine for the first weeks, with the doctor's letter. Check the medicine's UK legality before you pack it.

### Step by step: your approvals timeline

1. **Day you receive the offer:** ask about English requirements and ATAS in writing.
2. **8 to 10 weeks before you apply:** book your English test if you have not taken one.
3. **6 to 8 weeks before you apply:** apply for ATAS, if needed.
4. **4 to 6 weeks before you apply:** book the TB test.
5. **2 to 4 weeks before you apply:** collect your certificates and check the expiry dates.
6. **After approval:** check vaccinations and book a pre-travel check.

::: {.example title="A timeline that held up"}
A student gets his CAS in June for a September start. He books the IELTS test the same week and gets the result in nine days. His course needs ATAS, which arrives in 41 days. He books his TB test in July, gets a certificate, and applies for his visa on 28 July, which is after all three are in his hand. His funds were in place since May. The application takes three weeks, and the decision arrives in mid-August.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Booking the wrong test (not a SELT) because it was cheaper or closer.
- Taking the English test early and letting it expire.
- Applying for ATAS only after the offer is made, with a 30-working-day wait.
- Using a clinic that is not approved for the Home Office.
- Forgetting that a TB certificate has a validity window.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: GOV.UK for the approved SELT providers and the English requirement, the TB clinic list for your country, the ATAS guidance, and the NHS vaccination pages for students. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

- Write down which tests and certificates apply to you.
- Book the English test and ask about ATAS this week.
- Put the TB clinic and its appointment date on your calendar.
:::

## Flights, luggage and arriving on time {.chapter number="6"}

This chapter covers booking flights and arrival dates, what to pack, what you may and may not bring into the UK, how to carry money and documents, and what happens at the airport and at border control. By the end you should have a travel plan that fits your visa dates and your university's arrival period.

### What you need to know

Your visa dates, your university's arrival or enrolment dates and your first accommodation dates all need to line up. Arrive too early and you may have to pay for extra nights. Arrive too late and you may miss enrolment, orientation and, in the worst case, miss the deadline to register on your course, which your university must report to the Home Office.

::: {.plain title="Three dates to match"}
Your **visa start date** (when your permission begins), your **university arrival window** (when enrolment, induction and halls check-in start) and your **accommodation start date** (the first night you can sleep in your room). Book your flight for the day that suits all three, with a day to spare.
:::

### Booking flights

Book only after your visa is decided, unless the fare is fully refundable. If you must book early, buy a flexible ticket. Compare direct and one-stop flights to your arrival airport, because the cheapest route into a smaller airport can leave you with an expensive onward journey. London Heathrow, London Gatwick and Manchester are the main entry points for students from West and East Africa, and several airlines fly direct from Lagos, Abuja, Accra, Nairobi and Johannesburg [VERIFY: current direct routes]{.v}.

![An airport terminal. Check how you will get from the airport to your accommodation before you land.](images/GBR-04-c06-airport-arrivals.webp)

- **Arrival time.** A morning arrival gives you daylight to find your accommodation and a shop. A late-night arrival means taxi costs and limited food.
- **Luggage allowance.** Check the allowance on your ticket. Overweight bags are expensive. Many students ship less and buy bedding and kitchen items in the UK.
- **Connections.** Leave at least two to three hours for a connection that involves terminal changes.
- **Pickup.** Ask whether the university offers a free or low-cost pickup in the arrival weeks.

### What to pack

Pack for a cold, wet climate and a small room. Heating is on, but you will walk outside in all weathers.

| Pack | Leave behind |
|---|---|
| A warm waterproof jacket, a hat, gloves and a scarf | Bulky items you can buy cheaply in the UK, such as pillows and a duvet |
| Comfortable walking shoes and one pair of formal shoes | Large quantities of food that cannot be brought in |
| A UK plug adaptor (Type G) and a multi-socket extension | Electrical items that need a different voltage unless they are dual-voltage |
| Medicines with the doctor's letter and a copy of the prescription | Anything the UK bans or restricts (see below) |
| A small selection of cooking basics and spices | Cash beyond what you need for the first days |
| Copies of key documents, kept separately from the originals | Valuable jewellery |

::: {.src}
Compiled from university arrival guides and GOV.UK border guidance, October 2026. UK mains is 230 V and sockets are Type G.
:::

### What you can and cannot bring

Border Force can ask about your goods. As a general rule, meat, dairy and many fresh products from outside the UK and EU cannot be brought in. Some foods are allowed in small quantities, but check the GOV.UK pages before packing cooked meat, fish, fruit or vegetables [VERIFY: current GOV.UK rules on personal imports of food]{.v}. Medicines that are legal at home can be controlled in the UK. Carry them in the original packaging with the prescription, and check the UK's controlled drug rules before you travel [VERIFY: your medicine on the UK controlled-drug list]{.v}.

**Cash.** You may bring any amount, but if you carry £10,000 or more (or the equivalent) you must declare it [VERIFY: current declaration threshold]{.v}. In practice, you should arrive with a small amount of sterling and a working debit or travel card, rather than a large amount of cash.

### At the airport and border control

You will pass through immigration. Join the correct queue and have your passport, your CAS and your proof of funds and accommodation ready. Many student arrivals use the e-passport gates only if the passport and nationality are eligible, so be ready to speak to an officer instead. The officer may ask the course and university name, where you will live, how you will pay, and how long you plan to stay. Answer briefly and truthfully.

::: {.example title="A calm arrival"}
A student lands at Gatwick in the morning with his passport, CAS and accommodation confirmation in a folder. The border officer asks him which course he has been accepted for, where he will live and how he plans to pay for his living costs. He answers in two sentences each. He is waved through in four minutes and takes the train to his halls, where a welcome desk gives him his key and a map.
:::

### Step by step: arrival-day plan

1. **Download offline maps and the station or airport guide** before you board.
2. **Charge your phone** and check your eSIM or UK SIM is ready (Chapter 7).
3. **Keep documents in your hand luggage:** passport, CAS, visa decision, accommodation confirmation, funds evidence, vaccination record.
4. **Get through immigration** and collect your bags.
5. **Take licensed transport** (official taxi rank, train or coach). Avoid unlicensed offers in the arrivals hall.
6. **Check in** to your accommodation and send a message to your family that you have arrived.

::: {.warn}
Do not accept rides, SIM cards or "welcome packs" from strangers who approach you in the arrivals hall. Use the official taxi rank, the train or coach, or the university pickup. Never hand your passport to anyone other than a uniformed official.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Booking a non-refundable flight before the visa decision.
- Arriving before halls open and paying for an unplanned hotel.
- Packing medicines without the letter and prescription.
- Carrying valuables and documents in checked luggage.
- Taking an unlicensed taxi from the airport.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your university's arrival and pickup page, GOV.UK on bringing goods and cash into the UK, and your airline's baggage rules. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

::: {.next}
- Write your visa start date, enrolment dates and accommodation date on one page.
- Book flexible flights only after your visa is approved.
- Save a copy of your documents on your phone and in a cloud folder.
:::

## Staying connected from day one {.chapter number="7"}

This chapter covers phone plans, eSIMs, wi-fi, UK numbers and the apps you will need in the first weeks, including how to keep bank and university verification codes working. By the end you should have a connectivity plan that costs less than the airport SIM stand and never leaves you locked out.

### What you need to know

Almost everything in your first fortnight depends on your phone: university portals, bank apps, ride-hailing, maps, accommodation messages and, above all, verification codes. If your home number does not work reliably abroad, you can be locked out of the very accounts you need. The solution is to plan two things before you fly: **data when you land** and **a number you will keep**.

::: {.plain title="The two-number plan"}
Keep your home number alive for the banks and services that send it verification codes. Add a UK number (a SIM or an eSIM plan) for local calls, deliveries and UK services. Many students keep both for the first term, then decide which to keep.
:::

### Your options

| Option | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Data eSIM bought before you fly | Instant data on landing, no shop queue | Needs an eSIM-compatible phone, unlocked from your home network; data only in most cases. |
| UK prepaid SIM (pay as you go) | Local number, cheap calls and data | Usually needs a UK address to register and top up; buy at the airport at higher prices or in town. |
| UK monthly SIM-only plan | Lowest cost per gigabyte | Often needs a UK bank account and credit check; short contracts help. |
| Wi-Fi calling and messaging apps | Calls home at no cost | Needs good wi-fi; mobile data costs if you use it abroad. |
| Roaming on your home plan | A fallback for the first hours | Usually the most expensive option; check the daily cap. |

::: {.src}
Editorial comparison, October 2026. Prices change; compare current offers.
:::

![Checking a phone on arrival. Make sure your phone is unlocked and eSIM-capable before you buy a plan.](images/GBR-04-c07-sim-esim.webp)

### Check your phone first

Three quick checks avoid a surprise. First, is your phone **unlocked**, meaning it is not tied to one network? Second, does it support **eSIM**? Third, does it have **dual SIM**, so the home and UK numbers can work together? Look in your phone's settings, or ask your network. If your phone is locked, ask your home provider to unlock it before you travel.

### Verification codes without roaming

Banks, exchanges, social accounts and payment apps often send codes by SMS to your home number. If you switch the home SIM off, those codes may not arrive. Choose one of these options before you leave, and test it.

- **Keep the home SIM active** with a small plan, and use wi-fi calling or a roaming add-on to receive text messages.
- **Move to an authenticator app** where the service allows it. An app generates codes on your phone without any network.
- **Add the UK number** to your accounts as a secondary contact, once you have it.
- **Use a virtual number service** for sign-ups that only need a short-term number, such as Naara's verification numbers. Keep your main accounts on a number you control for the long term.

::: {.insight}
For your first 48 hours, a data eSIM such as Naara Data lets you land with working data, receive university and landlord messages and keep your home number free for your bank's codes. Once you have a UK address, compare a monthly SIM-only plan, which is usually cheaper for the rest of the year.
:::

### Apps and accounts to prepare

- **University portal and email**, with two-factor authentication set up before you fly.
- **Banking apps**, both your home bank and the UK account you plan to open (Chapter 11).
- **Maps and transport** (Google Maps, Citymapper or your city's transport app, and the National Rail or Trainline app for trains and coaches).
- **Messaging and calling** (WhatsApp, Signal, FaceTime) and a way for family to reach you.
- **A password manager** and a copy of your recovery codes, stored securely.
- **The NHS App and your GP's online service**, once you register (Chapter 18).

### Step by step: set up your connectivity plan

1. **Check your phone** is unlocked and eSIM-capable.
2. **Choose a data eSIM** for your arrival and install it before you fly, following the provider's instructions; activate it only when you land if the plan starts on first use.
3. **Keep your home number alive** and test an SMS code on wi-fi.
4. **Move key accounts to an authenticator app** where you can.
5. **After you land and have an address,** compare a UK SIM-only plan and decide what to keep.
6. **Tell family your new number** and how to reach you on wi-fi.

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Cancelling the home number and being locked out of the bank.
- Buying a UK SIM at the airport at three times the town price.
- Assuming an eSIM works on a locked phone.
- Leaving two-factor authentication to the first week, when you need it most.
- Using free public wi-fi for banking without a secure connection.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your phone's settings for unlock and eSIM, your home bank's rules for verification codes abroad, and the terms of any eSIM or SIM plan, including start date, data limit and tethering. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

::: {.next}
- Check that your phone is unlocked and eSIM-capable.
- Pick one authenticator app and move one important account to it.
- Choose your arrival data plan before you buy your flight.
:::

## Your pre-departure budget {.chapter number="8"}

This chapter gives you a complete pre-departure budget: every cost between accepting your offer and your first month in the UK, and a monthly budget for the first year. By the end you should know the cash you need before you fly, the amount you must keep to show the Home Office, and the cash you need for the first week.

### What you need to know

Chapter 1 gave you a high-level total. This chapter turns it into a worksheet you can fill in. Three rules keep it honest. First, never plan to use the money you must show the Home Office for the 28-day period as spending money before you apply. Second, add a 10 per cent buffer, because exchange rates and prices move. Third, assume the first month costs more than later months, because you buy deposits, bedding, travel cards and food stocks all at once.

### Build your own budget in five steps

1. **List your fixed costs first:** rent, phone, transport pass.
2. **Add food** by assuming a weekly shop and a few meals out.
3. **Add course costs:** books, printing, trips and software.
4. **Add the first-month extras** from the worksheet.
5. **Add a 10 per cent buffer** and compare the total with your funds and your income plans (Chapter 17).

::: {.example title="A first-year budget in a northern city"}
A student studying a one-year master's in a northern English city pays £5,000 as a tuition deposit before the visa, plus £220 for the English test and £90 for the TB test. At application she pays £558 for the visa and £1,164 for the surcharge. Her flight costs £650. In September she pays £600 for the first month of halls and a £250 deposit, and spends £190 on bedding, kitchen items and a warm coat. Her monthly budget after that is about £950, so over the nine months of her course she plans £8,550 on top of tuition, which sits inside the £10,827 she showed the Home Office, with a buffer.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Is my budget realistic?"}
- I have a number for every line in the worksheet.
- My budget uses my city's prices, not an average.
- I have a buffer of about 10 per cent.
- I know which money is reserved for the 28-day rule until I apply.
- I know how I will pay in the first week.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Forgetting the first-month spike, so the first month's cash runs out.
- Spending money that must be shown for the visa before you apply.
- Using the Home Office minimum as the whole budget.
