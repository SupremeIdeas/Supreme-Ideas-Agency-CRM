# Accommodation and arrival {.part number="III" sub="Where you will live, how you get there, and what to do in the first days so that nothing important is missed."}

## Student accommodation: halls, private blocks and shared houses {.chapter number="9"}

This chapter explains your housing choices as an international student, how much they cost, how to apply, and how to read a contract before you sign. By the end you should be able to choose a first-year home that fits your budget and your visa timetable, and avoid the scams that target students.

### What you need to know

Your first home in the UK matters more than any other arrival decision. It sets your rent, your commute, your social life and your safety. For most international students the first year is easiest in **university halls or purpose-built student accommodation**, because you can apply from abroad, bills are often included and the university can confirm your address for the visa and enrolment. The private rental market, with its references, guarantors and short viewings, is harder to use before you arrive (see the companion book *Renting in the UK*, GBR-05).

| Option | What it is | Typical features | Think about |
|---|---|---|---|
| University halls | Rooms owned or managed by the university | Single rooms, shared kitchens, many with bills and wi-fi included; some catered | Often guaranteed for international first-years who apply on time; contracts are usually 39 to 51 weeks. |
| Private purpose-built student accommodation (PBSA) | Blocks run by private companies, often near campus | En-suite rooms and studios, gyms, social spaces; bills often included | Usually more expensive than halls; check the booking and cancellation terms carefully. |
| Shared house or flat (HMO) | A private rented house with several students | Cheaper rooms, more independence | Needs references and a guarantor, and in some cities rooms are booked a year ahead. |
| Studio or one-bed flat | Your own space | Privacy and quiet | The most expensive option; usually needs proof of income or a guarantor. |
| Homestay or lodging | A room in a family home | Meals sometimes included, local support | Fewer legal protections than a tenancy; house rules apply. |

### What it costs

Rents are the biggest part of your budget. A national student survey in 2025 found that students who pay rent paid about £563 a month on average, with London the most expensive region at about £812 a month and Scotland next at about £663 [VERIFY: 2026 survey figures]{.v}. Halls and PBSA rooms often cost more than a room in a shared house but include bills, wi-fi and contents insurance.

| Cost | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Halls or PBSA room (outside London) | £110 to £220 a week | En-suite rooms cost more than shared-bathroom rooms. |
| Halls or PBSA room (London) | £180 to £350 a week | Contracts can run 40 to 51 weeks. |
| Shared house room (outside London) | £400 to £650 a month | Bills usually extra unless stated. |
| Deposit | One month's rent or about £250 to £500 in halls; up to five weeks' rent in a private let in England | Held in a protected scheme in private lets. |
| Booking or holding fee | £0 to £300 | Check what happens if your visa is refused. |

### How to apply from abroad

1. **Apply the day you accept your offer.** The best rooms and the guarantees depend on early applications.
2. **Choose by total cost,** not weekly rent: add bills, laundry and travel.
3. **Read the contract.** Check the start and end dates, the notice you must give, who pays for damage, what happens if your visa is refused, and whether the deposit is protected.
4. **Pay only through the official portal** of the university or provider, never to a personal bank account.
5. **Keep the confirmation.** Your university may ask for it for your CAS or visa application, and you need it at the airport.

::: {.warn}
Student housing scams are common, especially around July to September. If a landlord asks you to pay a deposit before you have seen the contract, if the price looks far too low, if they ask you to pay by gift cards, crypto or a money-transfer app, or if they say they are "abroad" and will send the keys by post, walk away. Real student accommodation is offered by the university or by named, registered companies.
:::

### Reading a student contract

A halls or PBSA contract is usually a licence or tenancy that lasts for a fixed period. The things to check are in the table.

| Clause | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Contract length | Does it cover the whole course, including holidays? Do you pay for weeks you are away? |
| Visa refusal | Can you cancel and get your money back if your visa is refused or delayed? |
| Guarantor | Does the contract require a UK guarantor? Is there an alternative for international students? |
| Rent in advance | Is rent due all at once or by instalment? |
| Bills | What is included, and is there a fair-use limit on energy or data? |
| Visitors and noise | What are the rules on guests, parties and quiet hours? |
| Damage and cleaning | What are you charged for, and how is it assessed at the end? |

::: {.example title="Choosing between two offers"}
A student has an offer of a £165-a-week en-suite room in halls, with bills included, and a £120-a-week room in a shared house, with bills and a monthly travel pass on top. The halls room costs £6,435 over 39 weeks. The house costs £4,680 over 39 weeks, plus about £70 a month for bills and £45 for transport, so about £5,745. The difference is smaller than it looks, and halls give her a guaranteed room, a quick start, a safe address for the visa and a welcome network. She chooses halls for the first year.
:::

::: {.checklist title="Before I pay for accommodation"}
- I have the contract in writing and have read the cancellation and visa-refusal terms.
- I am paying the official provider's own account or portal.
- I know the total cost for the whole contract period.
- I know who the guarantor is, if one is needed, and how to arrange one.
- I know the check-in date and what I need to bring.
- I have asked how to check out and how the deposit is returned.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Paying a deposit to someone who has not shown a contract.
- Booking a private room before seeing the area and the commute.
- Signing a contract that does not cover the visa-refusal case.
- Forgetting summer or holiday charges in a long contract.
- Choosing the cheapest option and finding that bills and transport add up.

::: {.verify}

## Arriving: your first 48 hours {.chapter number="10"}

This chapter walks you through the first two days: reaching your accommodation, collecting your keys, completing your university enrolment and checking your visa. By the end you should have a simple plan for each hour, a list of what to do first and what can wait, and a clear idea of who to contact if something goes wrong.

### What you need to know

The first 48 hours have two goals: **be safe and settled**, and **complete the compulsory steps** that the university and the Home Office expect. Everything else can wait for the first week. Do the compulsory items first, because your university must tell the Home Office if you do not enrol.

::: {.plain title="Compulsory first"}
Arrive in the UK on a valid visa, reach your accommodation, enrol with your university (in person or online, with your passport and your eVisa evidence), collect your student card, and tell the university your UK address and phone number. Each university sets its own deadline. Check the exact date in your welcome email.
:::

### Getting from the airport to your door

Plan the route in advance, because jet lag and heavy luggage make navigation hard.

| Route | Good for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| University pickup or shuttle | Heaviest luggage and first-time arrivals | Often free or low cost in September; book ahead. |
| Train or Elizabeth line / Underground | London and most big cities | Cheapest per mile; buy a ticket in the app or at a machine, and watch for a late-night timetable. |
| Coach (National Express, Megabus and others) | Long distances at low prices | Book online; arrive early at the coach station. |
| Official taxi or minicab booked in an app | Late arrivals, large bags | More expensive; use a licensed vehicle and check the number plate. |

Compiled from airport and rail operator guidance, October 2026. Prices vary.
:::

### Your first-day checklist

1. **Message home** as soon as you arrive. Share your address and a trusted contact.
2. **Collect your keys.** Take photos of the room, the furniture and any damage on day one, and send them to the accommodation team the same day. This protects your deposit.
3. **Charge your phone and connect to wi-fi.**
4. **Buy basics** at a nearby supermarket: water, food for two days, toiletries and a bedding set if you did not bring one.
5. **Sleep.** Your first full day will be better for it.

### Your first-week administration

| When | Task | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Collect your keys and photograph the room | Protects your deposit. |
| Day 1 to 2 | Enrol with your university and collect your student card | Required for your visa; the university reports non-enrolment. |
| Day 2 to 3 | Check your eVisa online and save your share code process | You will use a share code to prove your status. |
| Week 1 | Register with a GP or the university health service | You need it to see an NHS doctor (Chapter 18). |
| Week 1 | Open a UK bank account | Needed for rent, part-time pay and a UK payment card (Chapter 11). |
| Week 1 | Get a UK SIM or confirm your eSIM plan | Keeps you reachable (Chapter 7). |
| Week 1 | Attend induction and the library tour | Your course starts fast. |
| Week 2 | Apply for a student railcard or local travel pass | Saves money from the first trip. |
| Week 2 | Visit the international advice team | They answer visa, work and money questions for free. |

### Checking your eVisa and share code

Sign in to your UKVI account and confirm that your details, dates and conditions are right. When a university, bank or employer needs to check your right to study or work, you generate a **share code** from your UKVI account and give it to them with your date of birth [VERIFY: current share-code process and how long a code lasts]{.v}. Generate it yourself, never let anyone else log in to your account, and never pay for a share code.

### Staying safe on night one

Your first night is the most vulnerable. Arrive in daylight if you can, keep your valuables on you, use licensed transport only, and do not carry more cash than you need. Save the emergency number **999** (police, fire, ambulance) and the non-emergency **101** and **111** (NHS advice) in your phone. Tell your family which accommodation you are staying in and keep your phone charged.

::: {.example title="A calm first day"}
A student lands at 09:30, takes the airport coach to the city, and checks in at halls at 13:00. She photographs the room and sends the photos to the accommodation team, buys food and a bedding set at the nearest supermarket, and sleeps for four hours. At 17:00 she completes online enrolment, and the next morning collects her student card. By Friday she has registered with the university GP, opened a bank account, and made one friend in her kitchen.
:::

::: {.checklist title="First 48 hours"}
- I have told family that I have arrived and where I am staying.
- I have photographed my room and sent the photos to the accommodation team.
- I have enrolled, or booked a date to enrol, within the university's deadline.
- I have checked my eVisa details online.
- I have saved 999, 101 and 111 in my phone.
- I have food, water and a way to charge my phone.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Missing the enrolment date because of jet lag or a late flight.
- Not photographing the room, then being charged for existing damage.
- Taking an unlicensed taxi because the queue was long.
- Giving a share code to an unknown website.
- Leaving GP registration until you are ill.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your university's welcome email for the enrolment deadline, your UKVI account for your eVisa details, and the transport operator's page for the latest timetable. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

## Settling in: money, transport, food and the new normal {.chapter number="11"}

This chapter covers opening a UK bank account, getting around cheaply, finding food from home, and handling the culture shock that most international students feel in the first month. By the end you should have a working bank card, a transport plan and a few habits that make the first term easier.

### What you need to know

The first month is a project, not a holiday. A UK bank account, a travel plan and a social plan will do more for your wellbeing than anything you buy. Expect to feel tired, homesick and unsure of small things, such as how to greet people, how to use the bus and what the unwritten rules are. This is normal, and almost every international student goes through it.

### Opening a UK bank account

You need a UK account to pay rent, receive part-time pay and use contactless payments easily. International students can usually open one with a passport, an eVisa share code or proof of the right to study, a CAS or university letter, and proof of address, such as a letter from your university or your accommodation contract [VERIFY: each bank's current documents]{.v}.

| Type | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| High-street banks (Barclays, HSBC, Lloyds, NatWest, Santander and others) | Branches, student overdrafts, a full range of services | Some require an appointment, and approval can take days. |
| Digital banks (Monzo, Starling and others) | Quick app-based setup, instant notifications | Verification can fail with some passports; keep your documents ready. |
| Money-transfer accounts (Wise, Revolut and others) | Cheap currency exchange and multi-currency cards | Not always accepted as a "main" account for rent or wages; check first. |

::: {.src}
Editorial summary, October 2026. Banks change their offers often; compare current terms.
:::

::: {.plain title="Student account tips"}
Ask whether the account has a fee, whether there is an interest-free overdraft (international students are not always offered one), and whether you can set up a standing order for rent. Keep your debit card in a safe place, switch on card-freeze in the app, and never share your PIN or one-time codes with anyone, including someone who says they work for the bank.
:::


### Getting around cheaply

- **Student railcards.** The 16-25 Railcard gives a third off most rail fares. It costs £35 for a year or £80 for three years, and mature full-time students can apply with a form endorsed by the university [VERIFY: current price and rules]{.v}.
- **City travel passes.** Many cities offer student bus or tram passes at a discount. In London, students can apply for discounted Oyster photocards [VERIFY: current eligibility and discount]{.v}.
- **Contactless.** Tap a bank card or phone on buses and trains in most cities. Daily caps stop you paying more than a day pass.
- **Walking and cycling.** Many students walk or cycle. Check the local bike-hire and second-hand bike schemes.
- **Night safety.** Use well-lit routes and licensed taxis, and share your location with a friend.

### Food from home, and eating well

Larger cities have African and Caribbean grocers and markets, and supermarkets stock a growing range of international foods. Ask your university's African and Caribbean society, or fellow students, where to buy yam, plantain, garri, spices and palm oil. Shopping in bulk with housemates, and cooking in groups, saves money. Check the supermarket's reduced-price shelves in the evening.

![A market stall. Local markets are often cheaper than supermarkets for fruit, vegetables and spices.](images/GBR-04-c11-market.webp)

### The unwritten rules

Every culture has them. UK norms you will meet in the first week include:

- **Queueing.** Wait your turn, and do not push in.
- **Punctuality.** Arrive on time for lectures, appointments and social events, or send a message if you will be late.
- **Politeness.** "Please", "thank you" and "sorry" are used all the time, and small talk about the weather is a way of being friendly.
- **Directness in writing, indirectness in speech.** Tutors may say "that's an interesting idea" when they mean "I disagree". Ask for specific feedback.
- **Alcohol and social life.** Alcohol is part of many student events, but it is never required. There are many alcohol-free events, societies and venues.
- **Personal space and noise.** Quiet hours in shared housing are real and are taken seriously.
- **Asking for help.** Staff are paid to help you. Asking is normal and respected.

### Looking after yourself in the first month

Homesickness peaks in weeks two to six, often when the novelty fades and the workload rises. Call home at a regular time, join one society in the first fortnight, keep one routine (a walk, a gym session, a prayer or meditation time) and speak to the university's wellbeing team if low mood lasts for more than two weeks. Chapter 14 explains the support available.

### Step by step: week-one settling plan

1. **Open your bank account** and set up a standing order for rent.
2. **Get a travel plan:** a railcard, a travel pass or a walking route.
3. **Find your nearest food shops,** including one with the food you miss.
4. **Join one society** and one free event.
5. **Walk your route to campus** at the time of your first lecture.
6. **Learn three phone numbers:** your university security team, the international advice team and the local non-emergency police number.

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Waiting too long to open a bank account, then paying rent with fees.
- Using only cash or a foreign card that charges high fees.
- Staying in your room for the first fortnight because it is easier.
- Skipping the induction events that explain rules and support.
- Sharing your bank PIN, card or account with a new "friend".
