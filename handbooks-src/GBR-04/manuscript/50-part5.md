# Money, work, health and safety {.part number="V" sub="How to manage your money, work lawfully, use the NHS and stay safe, with the rules that matter most to international students."}

## Everyday money: banking, budgeting, tax and fraud {.chapter number="16"}

This chapter covers how to manage money month to month: a budget that works, the taxes and charges that apply to students, how to send money home, and how to avoid the scams that target international students. By the end you should have a simple money system and a list of the charges you can avoid.

### What you need to know

Student life is a cash-flow problem. Your money comes in large lumps (savings, a loan, family support, a scholarship) and goes out in small, frequent amounts. The students who do well treat money as a weekly habit: a short check, a simple budget and a rule about big purchases.

::: {.plain title="A simple rule"}
Pay fixed costs first (rent, phone, transport), put a fixed amount into savings, then budget what is left by week. Check your account every Sunday, in five minutes.
:::


### A weekly budget that works

Build a budget from your actual bank statement in the first month. A UK student money survey in 2026 put average student spending at about £1,142 a month, with rent the largest item at about £529, but averages hide a lot, and your city, room and habits will change the picture [VERIFY: 2026 survey figures]{.v}.

| Category | Rule of thumb | How to save |
|---|---|---|
| Housing | The largest share, often more than half | Share, choose bills-included, compare halls with houses. |
| Food | About £35 to £55 a week outside London | Plan meals, cook in groups, use reduced-price shelves. |
| Transport | £5 to £25 a week | Railcard, walking, cycling, travel passes. |
| Phone and internet | £10 to £25 a month | SIM-only plan; use university wi-fi. |
| Course costs | £20 to £60 a month | Library copies, second-hand books, campus printing credits. |
| Social and personal | £15 to £40 a week | Free events, society nights, student discounts. |

::: {.src}
Editorial rules of thumb from student-money surveys and university guidance, October 2026. Adjust for your city.
:::

### Taxes and charges that affect students

- **Council tax.** In England, Wales and Scotland, full-time students are normally exempt from council tax, and a house occupied only by full-time students is exempt too. You apply to the council with a certificate from your university, usually available from the student portal [VERIFY: your council's process and Northern Ireland rules]{.v}.
- **TV licence.** You need one to watch live television or BBC iPlayer. In a shared house, a joint licence may cover everyone, and in halls each room may need its own [VERIFY: current licence cost and rules for student accommodation]{.v}.
- **Income tax and National Insurance.** If you work, you pay income tax only above the personal allowance (£12,570 a year) and National Insurance above its threshold, through your employer (Chapter 17).
- **Fees and charges for services.** Avoid overdraft fees, card foreign-transaction fees and cash-withdrawal fees by choosing the right account and card.

### Sending money home, and receiving it

Use regulated money-transfer providers and compare the **total** cost: the fee, the exchange rate margin and the delivery time. Avoid informal transfer agents who ask for cash, and never accept a transfer from someone you do not know. If your family sends money to you, ask them to use a regulated provider and a bank-to-bank route, and keep the receipts. These records matter if the Home Office or your bank ever asks where your funds came from.

| Option | Typical advantage | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Bank transfer | Safe and traceable | Higher fees and slower delivery. |
| Money-transfer apps (Wise, Remitly and others) | Lower fees and a clear exchange rate | Check the receiving country's limits and the provider's regulation. |
| Mobile money and wallet-to-wallet | Fast and convenient | Often limited to certain countries and limits. |
| Cash transfer agents | Convenient in some places | Higher cost and weaker protection; avoid for large amounts. |

::: {.src}
Editorial comparison, October 2026. Verify each provider's regulation and fees.
:::

### Scams that target international students

Fraudsters know that international students are new, often anxious about their visa and short of time. These are the common scams.

| Scam | How it works | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Fake immigration or visa call | A caller says they are from the Home Office, the police or your embassy and that your visa is in trouble unless you pay | Hang up. The Home Office does not ask for payment by phone, gift card or crypto. Call the official number yourself. |
| Fake HMRC or tax call | A caller threatens arrest or deportation for unpaid tax | Hang up. HMRC does not threaten arrest. Report it to HMRC. |
| Fake accommodation | A listing with a low price and a request for a deposit before you see the room | Never pay before a viewing and a written contract. |
| Fake job or "task" scam | You are asked to pay for training, or to "like" content for pay | Genuine employers never charge you to start. |
| Money mule recruitment | You are offered cash to let someone use your account for transfers | This is money laundering and a crime. It can end your visa and your degree. Refuse and report it. |
| Fake scholarship or agent | A message asks for an application fee | See Chapter 2. |
| Fake bank message | A text or call saying your account is locked | Never click a link. Call the number on the back of your card. |

::: {.warn}
Never give your PIN, a one-time code, your password or your online banking details to anyone. A real bank will not ask for them. If you think you have been scammed, call your bank at once on the number on your card, then report the fraud to Action Fraud (in England, Wales and Northern Ireland) or Police Scotland on 101. You can forward suspicious texts to 7726 and suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk [VERIFY: current reporting channels]{.v}.
:::

### Step by step: set up your money system

1. **Build your budget** from your first month's actual spending.
2. **Set up your standing orders** for rent and phone.
3. **Open a savings pot** inside your account and put in a small fixed amount each week.
4. **Turn on notifications** for every transaction.
5. **Apply for your council tax exemption** and check your TV licence status.
6. **Choose a regulated way to send or receive money** and keep receipts.

::: {.example title="Spotting a scam in 60 seconds"}
A student receives a call from a number that looks British. The caller says he is a Home Office officer and that the student's visa has an "irregularity". To fix it, he must pay £450 by gift card within the hour. The student remembers Chapter 16, says that he will call the official number himself, hangs up, and tells the international advice team. They confirm that it is a scam and help him report it.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Spending the first month as if the loan or savings will last for ever.
- Using an account or card that charges high foreign fees.
- Forgetting to apply for council tax exemption.
- Sending money through an informal agent without a receipt.
- Letting someone use your account "to help a friend".

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your bank's fee guide, your council's council-tax exemption page, GOV.UK on TV licensing, and the money-transfer provider's regulation status. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

## Working while you study {.chapter number="17"}

This chapter explains how much you can work, which jobs are allowed, how to get a National Insurance number, how tax and payslips work, what you must earn at least, and how to spot a bad employer. By the end you should be able to find a lawful part-time job and protect your visa and your pay.

### What you need to know

Many international students work part time to cover living costs and to build experience. It is allowed, within limits. The limits are part of your visa conditions, and breaking them is one of the most serious things a student can do. Do not exceed them, even once.

| Situation | Usual rule | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Degree-level course, term time | Up to 20 hours a week | Counted Monday to Sunday, so two jobs add together [VERIFY: your visa's exact condition]{.v}. |
| Below degree level | Usually up to 10 hours a week in term time | Check your visa. |
| Official vacations | Full-time work usually allowed | Only for the vacation periods your university lists. |
| Work placement as part of the course | Allowed within the course rules | Ask the international advice team. |
| Self-employment, freelancing, running a business | Not allowed | Includes gig and delivery work as an "independent contractor" in many cases [VERIFY: gig work rules]{.v}. |
| Professional sportsperson or coach | Not allowed | |
| Student union sabbatical officer | Allowed as an exception | |

::: {.src}
Compiled from GOV.UK Student visa work guidance and university advice pages, October 2026. Always check your own visa and eVisa conditions.
:::

::: {.warn}
Check your own eVisa conditions before you accept a job. The hours limit is about **your** visa, not what an employer says is acceptable. An employer who asks you to work "a few extra hours cash in hand" is asking you to break your conditions and may be breaking the law too.
:::

![A cafe counter. Cafes and campus outlets are common part-time jobs, and campus employers usually understand visa limits.](images/GBR-04-c17-campus-cafe.webp)

### Pay, rights and the minimum wage

Employers in the UK must pay at least the National Minimum Wage. From April 2026 the rates are £12.71 an hour for workers aged 21 and over, £10.85 for 18 to 20, and £8.00 for under 18 and apprentices [VERIFY: GOV.UK national minimum wage page]{.v}. You are entitled to a payslip, paid holiday and a written statement of your terms. Employers must not charge you to start a job, take your passport, or pay below the minimum.

| Example | Hours a week | Pay at £12.71 an hour, before tax |
|---|---|---|
| Light part-time | 10 | £127.10 |
| Standard term-time limit | 20 | £254.20 |
| Full-time vacation work | 37.5 | £476.63 |

::: {.src}
Calculated from the National Minimum Wage rate of £12.71 for workers aged 21 and over, April 2026.
:::

### National Insurance and tax

- **National Insurance number.** It tracks your tax and contributions. You apply for it once you have a job offer or are about to start work, through GOV.UK. You may not need it to start work, because your employer can pay you with your details, but it is needed for your tax record [VERIFY: current application process]{.v}.
- **Income tax.** In most cases you pay tax only on earnings above £12,570 a year, and your employer deducts it. If you work only part of the year, you may be due a refund.
- **Right-to-work checks.** Your employer must check that you can work. You will give a share code from your UKVI account (Chapter 10), and the employer checks it online.
- **Payslips.** Check that your name, hours, pay rate, tax and National Insurance are correct, and keep them. They are also evidence of lawful work if you apply for a visa later.

### Finding a lawful job

1. **Start with your university.** Campus jobs, student ambassador roles, library and student union jobs usually understand visa limits.
2. **Use the careers service's job board.** Many local employers post part-time jobs there.
3. **Use reputable job sites** for retail, hospitality, tutoring and delivery roles, and apply for jobs with a clear contract.
4. **Ask your students' union** about on-campus jobs and about employer talks.
5. **Check the hours** against your limit before you accept.
6. **Sign a written contract** and keep a copy.

### Warning signs of a bad employer

- Paid in cash with no payslip.
- Asked to pay a fee or buy a uniform before the first shift.
- Asked to work more hours than your visa allows.
- No written contract, or one that changes after you start.
- Your passport or documents are held.
- A recruiter asks you to pay for "training" or "visa support".

::: {.example title="Counting your hours"}
A student works 12 hours a week in a campus café and takes a second job for 10 hours in a shop. Both are legal on their own, but together they total 22 hours, which breaks the 20-hour limit. A friend tells her. She speaks to the shop manager, reduces her shop hours to 8, and writes the new pattern in her diary. If she had not, the extra hours could have breached her visa.
:::

### Step by step: start work safely

1. **Check your visa conditions** and your weekly hours.
2. **Get a written offer** and contract.
3. **Prepare a share code** for the right-to-work check.
4. **Apply for your National Insurance number** if you do not have one.
5. **Check your first payslip** against the contract.
6. **Keep a log of your hours** each week.

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Counting hours by shift instead of by week.
- Accepting "cash in hand" work.
- Running a side business.
- Letting an employer hold your documents.
- Not keeping payslips.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: your own eVisa conditions, the GOV.UK Student visa work page, the National Minimum Wage rates and your university's international advice team. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

## Health and the NHS {.chapter number="18"}

This chapter explains how to use the National Health Service as a student: how to register, what is free, what costs money, how to get urgent help, and how to look after your mental and sexual health. By the end you should know exactly where to go for each kind of problem.

### What you need to know

When you apply for a Student visa you pay the Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS). It gives you access to the NHS during your stay, on much the same basis as a UK resident. Most care is free at the point of use, with some charges for things such as prescriptions in England and some dental treatment. The system is easier to use if you register early and know which service to choose.

::: {.plain title="Who to go to first"}
Not an emergency? Start with your GP surgery, or a pharmacy for minor problems. Need advice quickly? Use NHS 111 online or by phone. Life-threatening emergency? Call 999. Do not go to an A&E department for a minor illness.
:::

### Register with a GP

A GP (general practitioner) is your family doctor and the way into most NHS services. You choose a GP surgery near your home or campus, fill in a registration form, and wait for confirmation. Many universities have an on-campus health centre that you can join. You normally do not need proof of address or immigration status to register, though some surgeries ask for them, and they cannot refuse you on those grounds [VERIFY: current NHS England guidance on registration]{.v}. Register in your first week, so that you are not looking for a surgery when you are unwell.

### Which service for which problem

| Problem | Where to go | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A cold, minor cut, upset stomach or a mild skin problem | Pharmacy | Pharmacists give free advice and can recommend medicines. |
| A health concern that is not an emergency | GP surgery, or NHS 111 | Book online, by phone or in person. |
| Urgent but not life-threatening (for example a sprain or minor burn) | Urgent treatment centre or walk-in centre | Check opening hours online. |
| Serious injury, chest pain, difficulty breathing, severe bleeding | A&E, or call 999 | A&E is for emergencies. |
| Mental health crisis | NHS 111 mental health option, your GP, the university wellbeing service or 999 in an emergency | Samaritans: 116 123. |
| Dental pain | NHS dentist or NHS 111 | Dental care has charges; registration with a dentist can be hard, so ask early [VERIFY: charges and availability in your area]{.v}. |
| Sexual health | A sexual health clinic | Free and confidential in the UK. |
| Eyes | An optician | Eye tests are not free for most adults; students may find a university discount. |

::: {.src}
Compiled from NHS and university health-centre pages, October 2026. Services differ by area and nation.
:::


### What costs money

| Item | What you pay | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| GP visits | Free | You paid the surcharge already. |
| Hospital treatment and A&E | Free for most care | Some services have charges. |
| Prescriptions in England | £9.90 per item [VERIFY: current charge]{.v} | Prescriptions are free in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, and a prepayment certificate can save money in England. |
| NHS dental treatment | Charges by band | Check the current bands on the NHS website. |
| Glasses and eye tests | Usually charged | Check for student discounts. |

::: {.src}
NHS England and the devolved nations' health services, October 2026. Charges change each year.
:::

### Vaccines and staying well

Check that you have had the **MMR** and **MenACWY** vaccines, which the NHS recommends for students living in halls [VERIFY: current NHS vaccination advice for students]{.v}. If you have not, ask your GP surgery. Wash your hands, sleep and eat well. During winter, many students catch colds and flu. Stay at home when you are ill, to protect others.

### Mental and sexual health

- **Mental health.** Use the university wellbeing service and your GP. Help is confidential and does not affect your visa (Chapter 14).
- **Sexual health.** UK sexual health clinics provide free and confidential testing, treatment and contraception, including to international students. They do not share your details with the Home Office.
- **Emergency contraception and pregnancy.** Pharmacies and clinics can give advice. Your GP can explain your options and referral routes.

### Travelling home and travel insurance

Your IHS covers care in the UK, not the cost of care abroad or flights home. For trips home, buy travel insurance and check what happens if you must return urgently. Many students also take out contents insurance for their laptop and phone.

### Step by step: set up your health plan

1. **Register with a GP** in the first week.
2. **Save 111, 999 and the Samaritans number** in your phone.
3. **Find your nearest pharmacy** and urgent treatment centre.
4. **Check your vaccinations** and book any you need.
5. **Keep a short health summary** and your medicines list on your phone.
6. **Buy travel insurance** before your next trip home.

::: {.example title="The right door"}
A student wakes with a high temperature and a sore throat on a Sunday. He checks the NHS 111 website, which advises a pharmacy visit and rest, and a GP appointment if he is no better in two days. The pharmacist suggests paracetamol and fluids. By Tuesday he has recovered, and he has avoided an A&E queue.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Waiting until you are ill to register with a GP.
- Using A&E for minor illnesses.
- Assuming the NHS is free for everything, including dental and prescriptions in England.
- Hiding a health problem because of fear about the visa.
- Not buying travel insurance for trips home.

::: {.verify}
Check before you act: the NHS website for your nation, your university health centre, and GOV.UK on the Immigration Health Surcharge. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::

## Safety, rights and when things go wrong {.chapter number="19"}

This chapter covers personal safety, your rights, what to do if you are a victim of crime, how to deal with lost documents and emergencies, and when to call for help. By the end you should know the numbers to call and the first three steps for the most common problems.

### What you need to know

The UK is a safe place to study for the great majority of students. Like any country, it has crime, scams, harassment and accidents. Being prepared is the best protection. Most problems are easier to solve if you act quickly and ask for help.

::: {.plain title="Numbers to save"}
**999** for emergencies (police, fire, ambulance). **101** for non-emergency police matters in England, Wales and Scotland. **111** for NHS advice. **116 123** for the Samaritans. Your university's campus security number. Your embassy or high commission number.
:::

![A city street at night. Plan your route home and keep your phone charged.](images/GBR-04-c19-night-street.webp)

### Staying safe day to day

- **Out at night.** Plan your route home, use well-lit streets, keep your phone charged and share your location with a friend. Use licensed taxis, and check the number plate.
- **Drinks.** Never leave a drink unattended, and do not accept a drink from a stranger. If you feel unusually drunk or unwell, tell a friend or staff at once.
- **Phone and bag.** Keep them close in crowds and on public transport. Phone theft from hands near roads and stations is a common crime.
- **Home.** Lock your door, even when you are in the building, and do not let strangers follow you in.
- **Online.** Use strong passwords, a password manager and two-factor authentication. Treat unexpected links with suspicion.
- **Alcohol and drugs.** Drug possession and supply are crimes in the UK. A conviction can end your visa.
- **Knife crime.** In some areas, knife crime is a risk. Avoid arguments, leave if a situation escalates and do not carry a knife, even for protection, because carrying one is a crime.

### Your rights if the police stop you

If a police officer stops you, stay calm and polite. You have the right to ask why you were stopped, and to see the officer's identification if they are not in uniform. Tell the officer your name and address if asked. You do not have to carry your passport, but you may be asked to show your immigration status, which you can do with an eVisa share code [VERIFY: current rules on proving immigration status]{.v}. If you are arrested, you have the right to free legal advice and to have someone told of your arrest. Ask for the free duty solicitor and contact your university's international advice team.

### If you are a victim of crime

1. **Get to safety** and call 999 if you are in danger.
2. **Report the crime.** Call 101 or report online, and ask for a crime reference number.
3. **Tell your university.** The student services or security team can help and can arrange support.
4. **Get medical care** if you are hurt.
5. **Keep evidence:** photos, messages, receipts.
6. **Ask for victim support.** Victim Support offers free, confidential help [VERIFY: contact details]{.v}.

Reporting a crime does not put your visa at risk. Victims of crime are not treated as offenders.

### Lost or stolen passport, phone or documents

| Item | First steps |
|---|---|
| Passport | Report it to the police (get a crime reference), then contact your embassy or high commission for an emergency travel document. Tell your university and update the passport details linked to your UKVI account. |
| Phone | Lock it remotely, block the SIM and bank apps, and report theft to the police. |
| Bank card | Freeze it in the app and call the number on the back of the card. |
| eVisa access | Your eVisa is digital, so a lost passport does not delete it. Link your new passport in your UKVI account [VERIFY: process for linking a replacement passport]{.v}. |
| CAS or documents | Ask the university for a copy; the CAS is electronic. |

### Harassment, discrimination and complaints

Chapter 14 explained how to report racism, harassment and sexual misconduct. You may also complain to the university, and if you remain unhappy after its process, to the independent complaints body for your nation, such as the OIA in England and Wales [VERIFY: time limits]{.v}. Your students' union advice service can help you write and file a complaint.

### Immigration enforcement and your status

If someone from the Home Office contacts you, stay calm and ask for their name and a reference. Call the university's international advice team before you give any information. Never pay anyone who claims to be an immigration officer. If your visa is cancelled or curtailed, you may have only a short period to leave or apply for another visa, so act on the same day and get advice from a regulated adviser or solicitor [VERIFY: options and time limits]{.v}.

### Family emergencies and bereavement

If a family member at home is seriously ill or dies, tell your tutor and the international advice team. They can discuss emergency travel, extensions and what the absence means for your visa. Keep your return ticket flexible, and ask whether the university has a hardship fund.

### Step by step: your emergency plan

1. **Save the numbers** in your phone and write them on paper.
2. **Share your address** and your embassy's details with family.
3. **Back up your documents** in a secure cloud folder.
4. **Know your nearest A&E, police station and pharmacy.**
5. **Tell a friend** if something is wrong.

::: {.example title="Losing a passport"}
A student loses his passport on a train. He reports it to British Transport Police, gets a crime reference, emails his university's international advice team and asks the embassy for an emergency travel document. After a few weeks he gets a new passport and links it in his UKVI account. He never travelled in the meantime, and his visa status remained valid throughout.
:::

### Mistakes that cost people money or time

- Not reporting a crime because of fear about the visa.
- Walking home alone at night with an uncharged phone.
- Paying someone who says they can "fix" a visa problem.
- Keeping all your documents in one bag.
- Not telling the university about a serious problem.

Check before you act: your university's security and student services pages, the police in your area, your embassy or high commission, and Victim Support. Last verified: 19 October 2026.
:::
